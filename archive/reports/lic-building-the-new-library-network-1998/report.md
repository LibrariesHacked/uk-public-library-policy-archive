# 1 Introduction

## Building the New Library Network

> 'A UK-wide information network made available through libraries and implemented on the basis of a high-specification central core could do more to broaden and encourage the spread of information and communication technology skills among the population... than any other measure the government could introduce.'  
> — *New Library: The People's Network*, July 1997

The Library and Information Commission published *New Library: The People's Network* in 1997, setting out a strategy for a radical transformation in the character and importance of our public libraries. It proposed that they should be connected to a national digital network, giving them a fundamentally new role as managers of electronic content and gateways to a vast wealth of online information.

The government responded by asking the Commission to produce firm proposals to translate its vision into reality.

This is our plan.

It is divided into three sections, containing details and costed implementation proposals for each of the three strands of our programme.

### Network
We propose to develop a New Library Network which will initially be based on the Internet but which is capable of evolving into a dedicated broadband network if required. We suggest that local authorities should purchase kitemarked managed services based around a core user specification which will vary according to local need. This is compatible with the process being developed by the UK Education Departments for the National Grid for Learning.

### Content
We put forward a framework for defining, creating and managing the resources available on the Network. Libraries will offer access to educational and cultural material and take on an important new role as creators and developers of new digital content.

<!-- PAGE: 2 -->

### Training
We set out a rapid-action programme to equip the nation's librarians with new skills to handle information and communication technology, access databases and online information, and take on new roles as guides and instructors.

A development agency will help to bring the Network about by co-ordinating these three elements.

Central to our report has been the recognition that libraries must play a critical role in realising this government's ambitious plans for lifelong learning. By connecting to the National Grid for Learning, libraries will become a vital extension of mainstream online educational programmes. Easy access to a high-performance network will, in addition, encourage adults to use libraries for self-directed and informal learning and for reskilling, and will encourage a rapid acceleration in the amount of instructional material made available online.

As museums, galleries and other important national collections make their treasures available in digital form, the New Library Network will give easy access to more and more of our cultural heritage.

As the government's plans for remotely delivered public services reach fruition and all kinds of official information - including legislation and regulations - are made available in easily searchable electronic databases, libraries will become not just a gateway but a key interface between the individual and government at various levels. Furthermore, by seizing the opportunity to become managers and organisers of much local information, libraries will have the opportunity to carve out new roles as information hubs for their own local communities.

By supporting the government's strategy to reposition the UK to meet the challenges of the global information society, the New Library Network will rejuvenate the public library service for the next century. It will add value to the services's traditional role and build on its reputation as a local access point to the world of information and learning. It will also underpin social inclusion in the community and the wider economic prosperity of our society.

The Commission's programme is a clear route map to achieve nothing less than a revolutionary change in the nature and importance of our libraries.

It is our collective view that the recommendations set out here must be approached with imagination and resolution and be taken to their collective conclusion - implementation for the greater benefit of our citizens and the modernisation of our country in the information age.

*Matthew Evans, October 1998.*

# 2 Summary of recommendations on content, training and infrastructure

## Creating content

**2.1** The Content Creation Task Group included representatives from the UK library, museum and educational sectors, the Heritage Lottery Fund and, as observers, the New Opportunities Fund (NOF). Its main tasks were to advise the NOF on the priorities for the £50 million content creation programme, the eligibility criteria for individual projects and the ways in which the programme might be administered. Its work on these issues was underpinned by recognition of the NOF's legal obligation to ensure that projects have a demonstrable educational purpose.

**2.2** On priorities, the group recommended that:

- (i) the educational priority should be to support projects aimed at self-directed and informal learning, and projects which complement curriculum-specific material used in formal education and training. The NOF should not award grants to projects where the focus is solely on the production of curriculum material;
- (ii) three thematic strands should be established, relating to cultural enrichment, citizenship (including volunteering) and reskilling. These should form the framework for all decisions on awards;
- (iii) ideally these three strands should be funded equally. However, this principle will need to be applied flexibly in order to secure the maximum gain from the range and quality of projects funded;
- (iv) within these strands, around 60 per cent of resources should be targeted at UK-wide projects, 25 per cent at projects of country or regional significance, and 15 per cent at projects with a local focus but containing a national interest;
- (v) within this framework, the NOF should try to achieve a balance of projects both by subject area and by geography;
- (vi) the top priority should be to bring new material onto the Network. However, existing digital material could be eligible for funding where it forms part of a larger project or where it needs to be technically upgraded in order to allow it to be delivered through the libraries and schools networks;
- (vii) high priority should be given to the creation of integrated packs of learning material and multimedia applications;
- (viii) a high priority should be given to the digitisation of national treasures where they are incorporated into a wider lifelong learning or educational resource;
- (ix) content reflecting the multicultural aspects of UK society should be encouraged, with support given to indigenous and community languages and dialects;
- (x) the NOF should explore the scope for funding projects jointly with the Heritage Lottery Fund;

<!-- PAGE: 4 -->

- (xi) a modest sum should be set aside for the development of digital finding aids, but the NOF

should discuss the scope for more substantive provision in this area with the HLF.

## 2.3 On eligibility, the group concluded that:


- (i) applicants must demonstrate that the proposed content contributes to the educational

priorities set out above;

- (ii) partnerships between individual content-holding organisations should be encouraged;

- (iii) collaboration with private sector partners should also be encouraged;

- (iv) the content digitised should be made available free of charge on the New Library

Network and the National Grid for Learning. However, projects may secure revenues from valued added services or by exploiting the material in other ways ;

- (v) there should be a general presumption against funding projects where there is a viable

commercial alternative;

- (vi) applications should demonstrate that projects' standards are appropriate for the

requirements of infrastructure and training and will ensure maximum access, resilience and sustainability;

- (vii) projects should provide minimum matched funding of 10 per cent.  The value of

intellectual property should be counted towards the matched funding element;

- (viii) all applicants should demonstrate how projects address issues of access and social

exclusion;

- (ix) applicants should be required to demonstrate that various copyright and intellectual

property issues have been satisfactorily dealt with.

## 2.4 On administering the awards, the group concluded:


- (i) the NOF should ensure that it receives expert and independent advice in assessing

individual bids.  One option might be for the NOF to contract with an established agency to undertake this role. The recommendations of the agency might then be considered by an NOF Advisory Panel and the NOF board.  This is similar to the approach taken by other Lottery distributors;

- (ii) the agency should advise on bids for the whole of the UK and should include a built in

process to ensure that the interests of regions and the four home countries are recognised and taken into account;

- (iii) the NOF may also wish to contract with the agency to provide expert advice to applicants

on the suitability of potential projects;

- (iv) ideally, the NOF should aim to commit around 25 per cent of resources by the end of

1999, with a further 50per cent being committed in 2000;

- (v) once completed, projects should be evaluated by an additional independent authority or

organisation to ensure that they conform to the terms of the award and that targets have been met. Training

**2.5** The Training Task Group included representatives from the library and educational

sectors, including those responsible for developing the parallel training programme for <!-- PAGE: 5 --> teachers, and from the New Opportunities Fund. Its conclusions drew upon a training needs analysis conducted on its behalf by the British Educational Communications and Technology agency (BECTa), together with evidence from Wolfson-funded current public library experience.

**2.6** On priorities, the group concluded that the training programme should aspire to provide

advanced skills to as many librarians as possible and supply a clear route through from general to advanced training, based on identified core competences.

**2.7** For advanced training, the group concluded that, given the diversity of training  need, the

most cost-effective method would be for the NOF to rely on library authorities to specify their needs, rather than prescribing standards here.  Such training provision should equip key library staff to develop the skills they require to deliver the type of library service envisaged in New Library: the People’s Network.  Rather than accredit the providers of advanced training, it is proposed that library authorities should be required to demonstrate in applications that the training purchased by them will be of an assured and appropriate quality.  The NOF may wish to  seek expert advice from the agency on the suitability of the advanced training providers proposed.

**2.8** The group concluded that, in order to allow librarians to develop the information and

communication technology skills necessary to deliver the range of educational services identified in New Library: the People’s Network, all public library staff would need to possess certain core skills and  competences. These skills should encompass expertise in the use of ICT to support learning and in assisting members of the public to perform basic ICT operations. Training providers should demonstrate that they can address these needs, and should provide a mix of classroom training and interactive distance learning packages.

## 2.9 The priorities to which this core training should be directed are to:


- (i) support learning;

- (ii) enhance public access to information;

- (iii) support reader development; and

- (iv) assist access to public services.

**2.10** The group’s recommended option would be for training providers to develop core training

packages around a substantially enhanced version of the European Computer Driving Licence. These would need to address the specific librarianship functions identified above.

## 2.11 On eligibility, the group concluded that:


- (i) in order to secure the maximum impact from the funds available, NOF funds should apply

only to staff in regular employment in the public library service.  Staff who are employed only occasionally for relief work, or who work irregularly, should be excluded. Another measure to ensure the maximum impact from the funds available is to base awards to library authorities on actual numbers of staff employed, not their full-time equivalents;

- (ii) the NOF should invite applications from individual library authorities. However, once

funds have been allocated, recipients should be encouraged to maximise their purchasing power by developing consortia arrangements with other library and local education authorities;

- (iii) at least 15 per cent of the total costs of training programme should be met from the

authorities’ own resources. Salary costs of staff attending courses may be the matchedg funding element; <!-- PAGE: 6 --> - (iv) as the NOF appears highly unlikely to make funding available to provide staff cover for

those attending courses, no recommendations relating to this are made in this report. However, if the NOF board were to reconsider, there are certain circumstances in which such funding would be appropriate;

- (v) staff working in the School Library Service (as opposed to those working in school

libraries) should be eligible for funding. This should include staff employed in devolved or Local Education Authority-run services.

## 2.12 On administering the awards, the group proposed:


- (i) that the New Opportunities Fund should commission the design of a training needs

analysis tool for use by individual library authorities;

- (ii) that the NOF should secure professional advice from an appropriate agency to assess

individual bids;

- (iii) that a modest amount should be set aside to support cross-authority developments in the

provision of training. Network infrastructure

**2.13** The Network Infrastructure Task Group included representatives from central and local

government, the educational sector and specialist consultants.  It considered:

- (i) the technical and end-user requirements of the New Library Network;

- (ii) the process necessary to implement the Network;

- (iii) the likely costs associated with delivering the service, and how they might be met.

## 2.14 The group’s main conclusions were that:


- (i) the most cost-effective model is to develop a virtual private network together with local

management of ICT. In the short-term, national connectivity should be provided by the Internet, pending the development of commercial managed national services ;

- (ii) there are considerable financial and operational advantages in ensuring that the ICT and

networking procurement process complements as far as possible that being undertaken by the UK educational departments and the DCMS in developing the National Grid for Learning in schools and other institutions;

- (iii) while there is undoubted potential for many local authorities to develop services through

the PFI, a substantial injection of additional funds will be required if the network is to develop on a truly national basis;

- (iv) telecommunications tariffs form the largest element in determining network costs

(comprising about 50 per cent of ongoing costs). Securing a reduction in tariffs is an effective way of reducing costs. A discount similar to that currently enjoyed by schools but applied to broadband services could reduce the size of the funding gap by about 85 per cent. The network model

**2.15** The group costed a reference model which meets the current requirements of the UK's

4,000 libraries and their users and also provides an upgrade path for the future.  The model assumes the provision of in-library ICT in all branch and central libraries, with regional networking connecting libraries in one or more library authority and a backbone <!-- PAGE: 7 --> network for accessing national resources.  The model allows for controlled interconnection to other network communities (e.g. the Internet, the NGfL) and for sharing  physical capacity with other networks (e.g. local authority networks).

**2.16** For national connectivity, there is currently a choice of either using the Internet or

implementing a dedicated national backbone.  The costs of developing a dedicated national backbone are not justified by the likely level of demand or by the types of service currently available.  However, it is likely that commercial managed network services that could meet the requirements of the New Library Network for national connectivity, will become available in the near future.  Such services could offer considerable advantages, as a full-blown national network management agency would not be required and services could be purchased by local authorities on the basis of a published agreed tariff.

**2.17** It is not certain when and at what price such managed network services might enter the

market.  We therefore suggest that during 1999 the possibility of central government funding for national connectivity is pursued; but that decisions about the means by which this is implemented are delayed until the year 2000.  In the interim, national connectivity should be provided by the Internet. The procurement process

## 2.18 The group concluded that:


- (i) pending the development of commercially managed network services, a national agency

should kite-mark local, regional and national managed services, co-ordinate the development of the network alongside the National Grid for Learning, and exploit marketing and financial opportunities;

- (ii) procurement of ICT and regional networking should be undertaken by groups of local

authorities.  To secure economies of scale and to encourage inter-authority co-operation, it is proposed that library authorities should co-operate on a regional basis;

- (iii) libraries should specify their own user requirements based on specifications which

identify variations from, and/or additions to, national generic specifications.  The bases for such specifications are set out in Appendix 12 of the report.  They set out a range of service levels which will vary according to the needs of individual library authorities;

- (iv) local authorities should hold competitive tenders using kitemarked suppliers and/or

subject to the service meeting national standards; and

- (v) services would be implemented in a phased manner, region by region.  We suggest that

the procurement process be structured to include a pilot phase in its early stages.

**2.19** This approach is broadly comparable with that being taken by the UK educational

departments and the DCMS in developing the National Grid for Learning in schools and other institutions. We believe that every effort should be made to co-ordinate the procurement and development of the New Library Network and the NGfL.  This will provide clarity for the private sector by ensuring that it has a single consistent framework in which to provide managed services.  It will also encourage local authorities to co- ordinate the needs of schools and libraries in order to maximise the potential of the Network; and ensure that library users and schoolchildren are faced with compatible systems and services. Financing the Network

**2.20** The group conducted a comprehensive analysis of the likely costs of the network and how

these might be met.  This took into account the scope for developing the network through public-private partnership (PPP) and identified other sources of funding that might be available.

<!-- PAGE: 8 -->

**2.21** For the Network to develop in its fullest form (which would mean an average of ten

terminals in every library, linked to a regional network and a broadband national spine), the capital costs of establishing the initial physical infrastructure are estimated at £48 million. The annual revenue cost associated with the network are estimated - on current prices - at £160 million.  Taking existing resources and readily identifiable funding opportunities such as PPP into account,  and based on a number of assumptions which are set out in the main body of our report, we identified a funding gap of around £500 million over the first eight years of the Network.

**2.22** This figure does not take into account the scope for securing discounts arising from bulk

purchasing or the possibility of securing reductions in telecoms tariffs.  These could result in substantial reductions in the overall cost of the Network.  We estimate that if the Network were to secure reductions in telecoms tariffs of the same magnitude as those currently available to schools (i.e. 95 per cent reductions but applied to broadband services), then the funding gap would be reduced to about £70 million.

**2.23** Based on these figures, we estimate that, as a working sum, approximately £100 million

in additional funding is likely to be required over the next three years to deliver the Government's commitment to deliver the Network on a UK basis by the end of 2002.

# 3 Introduction to Part 1


It is the content delivered through the New Library Network that will indicate to both stakeholders and users how the public library has been repositioned for the information age. Moreover, it is through the delivery of relevant and accessible resources for lifelong learning that the library will play its part in wider government strategies to equip the UK as an information society. The range and quality of the content to be delivered will determine the success of the parallel investment in staff training - from the New Opportunities Fund and from other sources - in the New Library Network.

- The terms of reference for the Content Creation Task Group were as follows:

- To prepare advice for the New Opportunities Fund on how the content creation strand of

the New Opportunities Fund might best be designed and delivered.

- To develop eligibility criteria for the content-creation strand, taking into account the

statutory requirement for material to be of an educational nature, broadly defined as lifelong learning and education in its widest sense.

- To consider the funding priorities for the creation of new digital content, taking into

account educational need, commercial opportunities, the balance between regional and national priorities, and the views of other relevant content holders such as museums, archives and educational organisations. In the last year the government has recognised the need to create in the UK a 'learning society' to respond to the rapid and pervasive changes being brought about by the impact of new information and communication technologies. New technologies are already having a dramatic impact on government and society, industry and the economy - and on education and the way in which we learn. This process was acknowledged recently in the government's paper Our Information Age and in the government response to the Library and Information Commission's report on public library networking, New Library: The People's Network. The government's plans for the National Grid for Learning and the University for Industry will establish strategies for delivering new learning opportunities both into conventional education systems and, most importantly, to lifelong learners in less conventional settings. Through such strategies - of which the development of networked opportunities through libraries will be a key element - the UK will develop the skills base necessary for economic prosperity in the global information society and will enable more of its citizens to function fully in the emerging electronic information environment. Three areas of content for lifelong learning have been identified with which to initiate the creation of a lively New Library Network:

- cultural heritage and community identity;

- active citizenship and modern government;

- reskilling the nation.

## 3.1 Cultural heritage and community identity


A range of learning resources based on the rich cultural heritage of the UK forms the first area of proposed content. Cultural identity is the foundation of the community's self- confidence, particularly in today's multicultural and multilingual society. It has proved to <!-- PAGE: 13 --> be an area of interest and learning pursued with enthusiasm by every sector of our society. Cultural resources in the collections of libraries, museums, archives and many other institutions are highly suited to multimedia presentation. In addition, our cultural heritage enjoys an economic value at every level from local to international, and will be a key resource in the increasingly competitive cultural and service industries of the global information age.

## 3.2 Active citizenship and modern government


The relationship between citizens and government is changing. While transnational government changes through the evolution of the European Union, government in the UK will be transformed by a new focus on devolution to the home countries, the Council of the Isles, new patterns for government and administration in the English regions, and the new structures for London government. New technologies will bring new opportunities for citizen involvement. These, however, will require not only the development of skills in the use of information and communication technology but also a greater awareness and understanding of how society operates and of how a citizen can intervene for personal or community good. Public libraries have been recognised as having potential both as access points to government and other institutions and as a local route to the wider community. They will also be a local focal point for accessing sources of learning about government, citizenship and the operation of society.

<!-- PAGE: 14 -->

## 3.3 Reskilling the nation


People's capacity to benefit from the new opportunities of the information society will depend on their ability to make optimum use of new technologies. There is an urgent need to extend the skills base and to instil in everyone the capacity and the confidence to exploit these new technologies, so that all can play an active role in the community, the wider society and the economy. It is still the case that a substantial proportion of the population in the UK lacks basic functional literacy skills, while the emerging multimedia technologies require new skills in accessing and interpreting information and knowledge. This third content area envisages public libraries delivering the learning resources which will be needed to develop the literacies and skills which the citizen will need as a matter of urgency in the very near future. Part 1 of the report therefore addresses the strategic allocation of New Opportunities Fund resources for content creation, following the direction of the terms of reference. It covers the eligibility criteria for awards from the Fund, the priorities for funding, and the administration of the awards, and also addresses related legal and funding issues. It is supported by four appendices that consider the different issues in greater depth:

- Appendix 5: 'Digital content service development - a review of current activity'. This

describes the current digitisation activity in public libraries, museums, galleries and archives, both in the UK and overseas.

- Appendix 6: 'Learning needs and opportunities'. This outlines different views of the

concept of learning and how they might be adopted to inform decisions on the kinds of learning resource that might be developed with support from the New Opportunities Fund.

- Appendix 7: 'Heritage Lottery Fund position statement on digital content creation'. This

describes the Fund's current policy and outlines the potential relationship with the initiatives supported by the New Opportunities Fund.

- Appendix 8: 'Intellectual property issues'. This reviews those aspects of copyright that

need to be taken into account.

# 4 Eligibility criteria


The availability of £50 million to support the creation of information content for the New Library Network is likely to attract a large number of applications. The purpose of this chapter is to define the criteria that the New Opportunities Fund should use to determine which applications are eligible for support. The main criterion is whether or not the proposed content is educational in nature, and to determine this we describe what is meant by the phrase 'lifelong learning and education in its broadest sense'. We then go on to define the types of organisation that are eligible for awards and to discuss associated technical criteria, including the need to conform to technical standards and to ensure that intellectual property rights are dealt with satisfactorily.

## 4.1 Constraints on the New Opportunities Fund


The primary intention of the National Lottery is to improve the quality of life for people throughout the UK. As a consequence, the New Opportunities Fund is only able to support projects that promote the public good and which are not intended primarily for private gain. This may seem to constrain the role of private-sector providers of digital content. It does not, however, rule out their involvement in the initiative. Clearly, many private-sector providers will have much to contribute - particularly in terms of their expertise in content creation. Nor does it inhibit the production of digital content which may be acquired by library services and others as at present. Closely related to the previous constraint is the requirement that the content created as a result of this initiative should be made available free of charge to users through the New Library Network and the National Grid for Learning. This will to a great extent determine the range of organisations that will be eligible for awards under the content creation strand, and we discuss this in section 4.3. The Fund requires an element of partnership funding in all the projects it supports. We endorse this principle, but in section 4.4.3 we make the case for requiring only a low level of contribution from applicants, to encourage a high level of activity in what is still a relatively new area. The Fund has very limited powers to solicit applications. It can do so, however, when this is clearly the most effective way of achieving its objectives. We believe there are a number of support actions which will assist the creation of high-quality digital content and which will need to be undertaken very early in the life of the initiative, and in section 5.7 we argue that these might be supported through solicited applications.

## 4.2 Lifelong learning and education in its broadest sense


The concept of learning and education has changed considerably in recent years. The limited view which saw learning as a process which took place only inside the classroom has been replaced by a broader perspective which recognises that learning takes place throughout life. Formal education and training are important to a person's overall learning, but they are only part of a more extensive process. This shift in thinking is considered in more detail in Appendix 6.

<!-- PAGE: 16 -->

The development of the UK as an information society is reinforcing the importance of lifelong learning and stimulating the creation of a culture where there is a desire to learn. The government's recent vision statement Our Information Age, allied to the creation of the National Grid for Learning and the development of the University for Industry, will encourage this process. In this environment, formal education and training become part of a range of options for learning. Modern society attaches more importance to formal education and training than ever before, and that importance seems assured for the future. The difference is that more importance is now also attached to what New Library: The People's Network referred to as 'a personal learning path, whether in support of a career or an individual interest'. Lifelong learners include the widest possible range of people. They may be trying to improve their basic education or to obtain a vocational qualification, or simply pursuing an interest or a hobby. They may be at the beginning of life, learning how to read and to count; they may be developing the skills they need at work or for leisure; they may be retired and enriching their life through education. Public libraries exist to serve all these people. It is useful to distinguish three elements which together make up lifelong learning and education in its broadest sense:

- formal education and training;

- self-directed learning;

- informal learning.

### 4.2.1 Formal education and training


Formal education conforms to established curricula and structured courses: in schools pupils study the national curriculum and sit examinations for GCSE and Advanced or Higher Level qualifications; in universities and colleges students follow courses leading to degrees, certificates or diplomas. Most formal education still takes place in schools, adult education services, colleges and universities, but institutions are increasingly providing distance-education courses. Education is also delivered by a wide range of people outside these formal frameworks: parents teach their children; people train their colleagues at work. The significance lies in the fact that education is what we provide for others. Learning, in contrast, is what we do for ourselves.

### 4.2.2 Self-directed learning


Self-directed learning is what happens when someone pursues a deliberate or sustained interest in a subject outside, or in addition to, a formal course of study. It is characterised by the conscious nature of the activity: the individual sets out to learn about something; they do not acquire the knowledge accidentally or through experience. They may learn in isolation or as part of a more or less formal network of other learners. A growing amount of self-directed learning is vocational and design ed to improve the skills needed at work. Much self-directed learning, however, is undertaken for recreational purposes. It can be a solitary or a community interest, informing a person's perspective on life and sense of self-worth, and fulfilling the human desire to discover and to understand.

### 4.2.3 Informal learning


Informal learning begins at birth and continues throughout life. It is the process of learning to live within society. We all live in an increasingly complex society, and we need to learn how to navigate our way through the intricacies of life. Informal learning <!-- PAGE: 17 --> often arises as we pass through different stages in our lives: beginning a career, starting a new business, becoming a parent or dealing with a death in the family are all occasions when we have to learn. All of us learn through the process of social interaction with other people. We learn from our mistakes, and from the example of others. We learn what to do, and how to do it, from peers, family, friends and neighbours. Informal learning differs from self-directed learning in that it is seldom planned but is frequently undertaken in response to a need that arises as a result of day-to-day experiences.

<!-- PAGE: 18 -->

### 4.2.4 Eligible applications


Eligible applications will have to show clearly tha t the content will contribute to one or more of the above broad activities. The nature of the contribution will vary:

- Some materials will complement particular educational courses. Here a distinction needs

to be made between curriculum material - which we feel should not be supported by the New Opportunities Fund (see section 5.2.3) - and material that complements but is not directly attached to particular courses.

- Other material will contribute to self-directed learning within specific subjects or areas.

- In other cases the material will not be clearly identified with a particular course or a

specific topic but will be a resource that can be adapted for a variety of educational purposes. In such cases the applicant will need to show that the rationale for creating the digital content is to enable the material to be used to support education or learning - demonstrating explicitly the ways in which the content will contribute to learning of different kinds. Chapter 5, on funding priorities, provides guidance on the relative priorities that should be given to eligible applications that address different education and learning needs.

## 4.3 Types of organisation


The purpose of the content creation strand, as defined in the government's response to New Library: The People's Network 1, is 'for the digitisation of educational and learning material held to a great extent by public libraries'. The content will be delivered through the New Library Network and the National Grid for Learning. Public libraries are therefore likely to constitute the majority of applicants for support for content creation. Relevant material is, however, held by a number of other organisations, and it will be open to them to bid for awards under the initiative - either alone or in partnership with public library authorities (or the education and library boards in Northern Ireland). Many of these organisations will be in the public sector: museums, galleries and many archives contain material with considerable learning potential for use in the New Library Network. Given the educational and learning purpose of the content, and the close association with the National Grid for Learning, it is likely that educational institutions will also wish to contribute content to the Network. Funding made available through the New Opportunities Fund is intended primarily for public benefit and not for private gain. There are, however, some private- and voluntary- sector bodies that will be eligible to apply for funds. Professional and learned societies, for example, already play an active role in supporting education, training and self-directed learning, and they are likely to be able to contribute a considerable amount to the overall stock of digital information content. The voluntary sector is also recognised as an essential participant in the government's strategy to overcome social exclusion, and can do much to contribute to the development of learning content that will help to overcome social problems. There is also scope for involvement by other areas of the private sector, particularly in partnership with the public or voluntary sector, provided they can demonstrate that the material will support formal education, self-directed learning or informal learning. It must also be the intention to create content that will be made available through the New Library Network and the National Grid for Learning, where it must be made available free of charge at the point of use.

### 4.3.1 Collaboration and partnership

<!-- PAGE: 19 -->

There is much that can be gained from a partnership approach to the creation of information content, and applicants should therefore be actively encouraged to work with others in developing applications for funds. Joint approaches can lead to the exploitation of different types of resource and of different skills and approaches, with the result that the whole is greater than the sum of the parts. Partnerships of this kind can also lead to deeper understanding of the needs of different sectors, and a partnership formed for a single project may lead on to a more enduring relationship. In some cases the partnership will consist of groups of similar organisations within a particular geographical area. The devolution of powers to the home countries and the growing awareness of the need for regional development suggest that geographical groupings of this kind will have much to contribute in the process of creating digital content with a regional focus. In other cases, different types of organisation might come together to share resources and expertise: museums and archives could form an association to submit an application to create content to support learning. Partnership is also possible across sectors. A public library might join with a voluntary- sector organisation to prepare an application for funds. In the case of partnerships with private-sector bodies, however, it will be necessary for applicants to conform to two criteria:

- they must demonstrate that the resulting information content will be made available

through the New Library Network, free of charge at the point of use;

- the application must be for content that will promote public good and which is not

intended primarily for private gain. However, projects may secure revenues from value-added services, and there should be a general presumption against funding projects for which there is a viable commercial alternative.

## 4.4 Technical eligibility


The New Opportunities Fund must ensure that applications for support:

- conform to agreed technical standards;

- take account of intellectual property rights;

- are backed by matched funding;

- conform to agreed pricing arrangements.

### 4.4.1 Technical standards


To ensure interoperability across the Network, applicants must be able to demonstrate that the information products that will be created will conform to national and international standards for data format, metadata (content descriptions and access information) and project management. An essential initial task for the New Opportunities Fund or its agent will be to establish in advance what standards should be followed. In this it should work in consultation with other organisations, such as the Joint Information Systems Committee of the Higher Education Funding Councils, and with the managers of other networks - particularly the National Grid for Learning. A specification of technical standards to be followed should accompany each call for proposals.

### 4.4.2 Intellectual property rights

<!-- PAGE: 20 -->

The rights associated with intellectual property are quite complex, and those relating specifically to digital information are currently subject to considerable discussion and development. Appendix 8 provides a succinct guide to the issues. To be eligible for consideration, applicants must be able to demonstrate that they have taken the various intellectual property issues into account: in particular, that they own or have made arrangements to secure appropriate rights to use the material under consideration and to protect their own rights in a manner consistent with the overall objectives and constraints imposed on the use of this material. One of the immediate tasks of the Fund or its agent should be to draw up eligibility guidelines on intellectual property for use by applicants. Another should be to commission the preparation of standard licences for use with rights holders. This is considered in more detail in section 7.1.1

### 4.4.3 Matched funding


The New Opportunities Fund is obliged to require applicants to contribute to the overall cost of the project, to demonstrate the applicant organisation's commitment. This requirement needs to be balanced against the need to stimulate and encourage content creation projects in what will be a new area of activity for many organisations. Many eligible bodies will have no previous experience of creating digital content. They will not, therefore, be able to draw on existing resources or established flows of expenditure; rather, the activity will be undertaken for the first time, using resources drawn from elsewhere in the organisation. It is important that the matched funding requirement does not deter the submission of potentially good proposals. We recommend, therefore, that the requirement for matched funds be kept low - initially at least - to encourage organisations to begin the process of digital content creation. We recommend further that the New Opportunities Fund should follow the practice of the Heritage Lottery Fund, which has set the minimum contribution from applicants at only 10 per cent of the cost of the project, though applicants for support of large projects are expected to contribute 25 per cent. Given the need to bring content rapidly to the Network and the fact that many potential applicants are at an early stage in developing their capacity, we recommend that the New Opportunities Fund require all applicants to contribute only a minimum of 10 per cent of the total cost of the project. The nature of the resources used by applicants to match the Fund's award will vary. In some cases the applicant will be able to match New Opportunities Fund resources with a direct financial contribution. In others cases the grant will be matched in kind, by staff or other resources. Accepting staff costs as an element in matched funding may make it difficult to establish joint projects with the Heritage Lottery Fund. We feel, however, that this is not sufficient reason to exclude such projects. Applicants should also be able to contribute the value of the intellectual property rights residing in the information itself. This is potentially a difficult contribution to manage, as so much depends on the valuation placed on these rights. We therefore recommend that the New Opportunities Fund should adopt the approach which has been developed and operated successfully by the Scottish Cultural Resources Access Network. This is outlined in section 6.5.1.

### 4.4.4 Pricing arrangements


To be eligible for the scheme, applicants must be able to demonstrate that the pricing arrangements for material created through the scheme will not contravene the two basic principles:

- the material produced through support from the New Opportunities Fund should be for

public benefit as opposed to private gain;

- the material should be made accessible through the New Library Network and the

National Grid for Learning and should be available free of charge at the point of use.

<!-- PAGE: 21 -->

This does not mean that content creators are prohibited from levying a charge for the material created when it is used outside the New Library Network. Indeed, on grounds of sustainability, it may be desirable to identify revenue streams that could be used to sustain the resource or to support further content creation. One way of doing this would be to add value to the digital content once it has been created for use on the Network and then to market it commercially. This should not, however, restrict the availability of the original content on the New Library Network. Also, any such activity should not contravene more general limitations on the commercial activities of local authorities, such as those set out in the Local Authorities (Goods and Services) Act 1970.

# 5 Priorities for funding


Having established which applications are eligible for funding, the New Opportunities Fund (or the body to which it delegates the work) will have to choose between competing applications. This chapter recommends the priorities that should inform its choices.

## 5.1 Learning needs


The focus should clearly be on materials that will contribute to lifelong learning and education in its broadest sense. Within that general definition, the New Opportunities Fund should give priority to material that will support self-directed and informal learning, and to material which may be used to complement curriculum-specific material used in formal education and training. The purpose is to support the creation of learning content that will be available through the New Library Network and the National Grid for Learning. It is therefore important to reflect the ways in which people use public libraries to support their learning. People have traditionally turned to public libraries as a source of information and knowledge that contributes to their self-directed learning. This mode of learning is likely to become increasingly important in the coming years, and it therefore makes sense to ensure that there is an adequate supply of digital material that will support it. Similarly, people have turned to public libraries as a resource for informal learning - whether to borrow a book on parenting or to obtain information on their eligibility for local council services, for example. Again, material to support these informal learning needs should be readily available through the Network. Many people also use public libraries to access material that will complement formal education and training, and this will be an important function of the New Library Network. To maximise the opportunities to create material that can be used by learners through the New Library Network, however, this fund should not be used to create curriculum-specific material for formal education. There are a number of alternative sources of support for the creation of such material. The Electronic Libraries Programme, for example, is designed to meet the need for content creation in the higher education sector, and the National Grid for Learning provides a framework for the creation of digital material that will support the national curriculum in schools. Digital materials also offer new opportunities to overcome barriers to learning. Through disability, geographical isolation or social exclusion, many groups within the community were previously unable to make full use of the opportunities for learning that were open to others. Networked digital resources available through a wide variety of outlets can provide otherwise disadvantaged people with access to a rich stream of learning material. All applicants for funding should address issues of access and exclusion. It will also be important for applicants to demonstrate to the New Opportunities Fund that they have the skills and expertise required to create materials that will make a significant contribution to learning. Certainly, applicants should be able to demonstrate that they understand the ways in which content contributes to learning, as described in Appendix 6.

## 5.2 Types of material

<!-- PAGE: 23 -->

There is a wide range of material that will be eligible for support by the Fund. We believe that some types of material should be given a higher priority than others.

### 5.2.1 Resource discovery


Many people will discover digital content for the first time through  the New Library Network. Others, through the use of the Network, may obtain access to resources of which they were not previously aware. It will therefore be important to provide effective guides both to material that already exists in digital form and to the newly created materials. One of the major criticisms of the World Wide Web is that, even though it contains a wealth of valuable material, much of it is inaccessible to most of the users because they are unaware that the material exists and they are not guided towards it. Search engines provide a means of rectifying this situation, but they are a poor substitute for a systematic approach to the identification and description of digital materials. In contrast, in the academic sector, the Joint Information Systems Committee of the Higher Education Funding Councils has supported the development of a number of guides to resources, and these have done much to develop awareness and use of digital collections. Such guides to resources may be one stage removed from digital content designed to provide direct support to learning, but they are the key to unlocking the rich learning resources that already exist or will become available. We believe, therefore, that a modest portion of the fund should be given to the creation of digital finding aids that can guide users to the material they need. These can range from guides and gateways that provide links to digital collections, through to digital catalogues of collections and indexes to individual items. All should make it easier for people to identify and locate material that they require. Such aids, if widely available, will help to open up access to the collections of information held in public libraries, archives, museums and other organisations. Such guides and finding aids as may be funded by the New Opportunities Fund should complement those produced with support from the Heritage Lottery Fund. The New Opportunities Fund should discuss the scope for more substantive provision in this area with the Heritage Lottery Fund. The creation of such guides to resources is likely to absorb only a small proportion of the overall fund, yet the return on the investment through improved awareness of and access to digital collections will be considerable.

### 5.2.2 Individual resources


Many individual resources have an intrinsic cultural value, and their reproduction in digital form is worthy of support. Individual items from a museum collection would be one example; manuscripts from an archive would be another. In some cases the items might be grouped around a theme - an aspect of local history, perhaps, or the work of a local artist. In others they would stand alone on their own merit. The value of these individual resources will be enhanced if they are described or structured in consistent ways, so that they can easily be located and understood. Individual resources like these are likely to be of greatest value when they are used as complementary materials to support structured learning. To justify support by the New Opportunities Fund, applicants should be able to demonstrate how such free-standing digital resources will contribute to the satisfaction of learning needs.

<!-- PAGE: 24 -->

Some items are sufficiently valuable in their own right to be regarded as national treasures. Providing access to the national stock of such treasures would add much to the value of the New Library Network. We feel, therefore, that a high priority should be given to the digitisation of such treasures. For projects supported by the New Opportunities Fund, the primary purpose of their digitisation should be lifelong learning and education. Digitisation of national treasures will therefore most likely take place where they are incorporated into a wider lifelong learning or educational resource.(We clarify what we mean by 'national' in section 5.5.)

### 5.2.3 Learning resource packs


In general, a high priority should be given to applications for funds to produce integrated packs of learning resource material. The essential characteristic of these packs is that they add value to the basic digital resources, and in so doing they increase the potential of the digital content. Such a pack might comprise a collection of plans, drawings, photographs and documents that illustrate the development of a new town, for example. Learning resource packs can support a range of different learning needs. They can make a significant contribution to self-directed learning, with users simply accessing the material or working through it interactively, perhaps shaping the structure of the overall package so that it meets the distinctive needs of the individual. Some packages of this kind enable the user to contribute information of their own, adding their experience to the basic resource. Some learning resource packs are tied to specific aspects of a syllabus or are designed to support particular courses of education and training. Packs of this kind are specifically excluded from this initiative, because curriculum-based content will be delivered as part of the managed services provided for the National Grid for Learning The New Opportunities Fund should give a high priority to the production of learning resource packs and multimedia applications. They will require more effort in their construction than will the digitisation of individual resources, but they are likely to make a greater impact on learning needs, by complementing materials used in the formal education system and as a resource to support self-directed and informal learners.

### 5.2.4 Bringing existing digital content to the Network


If the Network is to take off satisfactorily, it will need to achieve a critical mass as soon as possible. One way to assist this is to bring existing digital material to the Network. Appendix 5 shows that a good start has been made, but that much more needs to be done. It will not be open to the New Opportunities Fund to support the purchase of existing digital material from commercial suppliers. However, much valuable material has already been created for the public good using public funds. For example, all the national libraries have created valuable digital resources that could usefully be made more widely available and accessible. The Fund should consider supporting projects that will bring such material to the Network as part of a newly created learning package. This will ensure that, as the New Library Network is rolled out, there is an early supply of relevant material which public librarians can use to stimulate interest in and experience of the Network. It may be necessary to adapt existing material so that it conforms to agreed technical standards and thus becomes usable throughout the Network. The Fund might reasonably be expected to contribute to the cost of technical adaptation, as, once acquired, the resource would then be available for use throughout the New Library Network and the National Grid for Learning. This will extend the benefit gained from the initial public investment.

<!-- PAGE: 25 -->

In the short term the Fund should give priority to applications of this kind which incorporate existing publicly funded digital resources.

### 5.2.5 Exploiting multimedia potential


Information can be presented in many different forms: as text, graphically or audibly. It is difficult to express a preference for one format over the others: much will depend on the needs of the user. The nature of the Network is such that it will enable information to be presented in multiple formats, combining text, pictures and sound. Such multimedia resources can be used to overcome some of the accessibility problems experienced by people with visual impairments, for example. Other things being equal, priority should be given to applications that exploit this multimedia capability.

## 5.3 The range of subject areas


Public libraries have always been, and will remain , social organisations, funded to advance social goals and objectives. The New Opportunities Fund might therefore legitimately take a view on the objectives of the lifelong learning and education for which material will be used. Given both the social purpose of public libraries and the goals of Lottery funding, it seems reasonable to recommend subject priorities in terms of the government's stated social, educational and economic policy objectives. The New Library Network will make a particular contribution to three of these social policy objectives:

- cultural enrichment;

- the modernisation of government and the encouragement of active citizenship;

- reskilling the nation to meet the challenge of an information society.

Priority should be given to applications for digital content to support learning in each of these three areas. There follows an outline of the possibilities in these three areas. This is meant to be illustrative, not prescriptive, and gives an insight into the kinds of learning resource which could emerge from this funding opportunity and the wide range of partner participants who could support their creation.

### 5.3.1 Cultural enrichment


Cultural enrichment encompasses a wide range of issues. At one level it is concerned with providing access to and developing the nation's cultural assets and its heritage, so that they can be used for lifelong learning. In this context it is necessary to draw a distinction between the content creation activity supported through this initiative and the work of the Heritage Lottery Fund. The Heritage Lottery Fund supports the digitisation of cultural and heritage material, but does so primarily in order that it may be better preserved and conserved, with enhancing access only a secondary aim. In contrast, the New Opportunities Fund supports the creation of content so that it can be used to support learning. This distinction, and the way it can be managed, is discussed in more detail in Chapter 6. At another level, cultural enrichment is concerned with r eflecting and projecting the cultural and linguistic diversity that exists within the UK. In this area, funded content might include a range of resources which inform, share and disseminate understanding of our diverse cultural heritage - for pleasure, for community self-confidence and, increasingly, for economic prosperity. Such resources would be relished by the independent learner, shared by local and national interest groups, and provide a wealth of opportunity for adult education providers and schools. Themes here might include:

<!-- PAGE: 26 -->

- Our digital heritage: local, regional and national history expressed in characteristic

objects, artefacts and archives selectively themed and presented with research findings, analysis and cross-referenced background in a way that will permit interpretation and enjoyment at different levels or abilities.

- Our community identity: local histories brought together from across the UK to form a

rich patchwork of personal and community identity in map and manuscript, photograph and archive film, and voice and other sound recordings.

- Our creative experience: the story of UK creative expression in the visual and fine arts,

the performing arts, fashion and design, architecture, film, television and the media - all as represented in the collections, resources, expertise and activities of cultural institutions, learned societies, arts bodies, libraries and community arts centres. In this area, partners at national and local level would include public libraries and archives, the British Library and other national libraries, museums and galleries, local history groups and collectors, learned societies, universities, the media, arts bodies and community arts centres.

### 5.3.2 Citizenship in a modern state


Enabling citizens to participate in modern government is another major challenge which the New Library Network can help meet. It can help to develop the concepts of citizenship and participation, both of which underpin the government's plans for the reform of central as well as local government. Critical changes in governance in Scotland and Wales, with the new Scottish Parliament and Welsh Assembly, make this a particularly opportune time for libraries to take a leading role. The government has well-advanced plans to develop remotely delivered public services, and the New Library Network will allow public libraries to play a key role in providing access to them. By providing open and free access to networked services, the New Library Network has the potential to ensure that minority groups within the community - notably disabled people and people from ethnic and other minority communities - are not disenfranchised. In this area, funded content might include a range of learning resources to equip the citizen for modern governance. Such resources would benefit the independent learner, help local community groups and charitable organisations, be used by adult education services, and complement the teaching of citizenship and participation in schools. Themes here might include:

- The evolving citizen: government services - what they are, how they operate, how you can

access them; how communities work; political parties and pressure groups; how to organise for your community or group; how to raise funds and realise and manage your objectives; the changing rights, responsibilities and opportunities of citizens in relation to new structures and forms of government; access to and freedom of information in a society built on communications and newly subject to the European Convention on Human Rights.

- The learning citizen: the 'learning for life' needs of different sectors of the community -

young adults, children, parents and carers, elderly people; handling issues like health, welfare and personal well-being, finance, education and training, work and retirement, leisure and learning.

- The safe citizen: managing personal family life at key stages - birth or bereavement,

homelessness and housing, education, retirement, leisure; personal financial control, <!-- PAGE: 27 --> benefits, pensions, savings and investments; community safety, transportation, the environment, and care in the local community. In this area, potential partners at national and local level would include libraries; voluntary-sector organisations (issue- or sector-based); government and local authority departments such as the youth service, community development, housing and social services; the education sector; community organisations and their national federations; information and advice agencies; and consumer organisations.

### 5.3.3 Reskilling the nation


Reskilling the nation to meet the needs of the information society implies equipping everyone with the levels of competence needed to prosper in a society which makes increasingly intensive use of information and new technologies at work and in daily social life. This is more than a question of acquiring skills in information and communication technology: many people still lack basic functional literacy skills, but the New Library Network can play a major role in combating social exclusion by helping people to acquire all the core skills they need. Reskilling the nation will also depend on enhancing awareness of training to develop specialist work-related skills, and access to it. A key priority will be to provide a bridge to the evolving University for Industry and the information networks that support it. In this area, funded content might include a range of resources to enable people to develop the literacy, information and communication skills and the technological awareness to function confidently and creatively in the emerging global information society and economy. Such resources would benefit individuals developing skills for work or for personal and shared use, complement informal learning in an adult education course, or, selectively, help with formal education in school and college. Themes here might include:

- The conventional literacies: fulfilling an increasing and ever more urgent need to combat

illiteracy and develop fluency in the core skills of reading, writing and numeracy.

- The new literacies: enabling access, interpretation, understanding and exploitation of

visual, multimedia and Internet communication technologies, overcoming ' techno-fear'.

- The creative literacies: using the new literacies for personal, family and community

benefit and maximising the opportunities for integration and social inclusion in a potentially alienating environment; developing skills in word processing, graphic expression, and database and Web site design. In this area, potential partners at national and local level would include libraries, training organisations, adult education and other further education providers, Training and Enterprise Councils, and local authority departments such as education and economic development. The three broad areas above should provide the basic shape for the information content made available through the New Library Network, and the New Opportunities Fund should reflect this in its priorities for the award of grants.

## 5.4 Partnerships


The concept of partnership lies at the heart of the New Library Network. Public libraries are encouraged to work with each other and with other agencies in the community. There is particular scope for cross-sectoral partnerships between libraries, museums, archives, schools and other educational establishments, and with the voluntary and private sectors.

<!-- PAGE: 28 -->

We therefore feel that priority should be given to applications that are submitted on behalf of two or more organisations or which demonstrate, at the very least, that other organisations have been fully consulted at the planning stages. All applications should demonstrate that the applicants fully understand the needs of learners working within the context of the public library service.

## 5.5 Local, regional or national focus


There is potential for a dilemma to arise from the desire to produce material that reflects local and regional needs and the desire also to maximise the value of the Fund by supporting material which is of relevance throughout the UK. Our view is that there should be three thematic strands covering cultural enrichment, citizenship in a modern state and reskilling. Ideally these three strands should be funded equally. However, we believe this principle should be applied flexibly, in order to secure the maximum gain from the range and quality of projects which are submitted for funding. Within these strands around 60 per cent of resources should be targeted at UK-wide projects, 25 per cent at projects of country or regional significance and 15 per cent at projects with a local focus but with the potential for national interest. Material that has a uniquely local focus should be given a lower priority. In these cases applicants should be required to show that the material, when available through the Network, will make a significant contribution to the UK-wide collection of information. Material that has a local focus but no national interest should not be funded through this initiative.

## 5.6 Linguistic and cultural identity


There is a strong case to be made for the Fund to support the digitisation of material that reflects the rich variety of our linguistic and cultural identity. In the case of Wales, the Welsh Language Act 1994 places on Welsh local authorities a duty to make information available in both English and Welsh, and the Fund should take this fully into account when assessing applications from Wales. In addition, the Fund should take account of the need to support indigenous and other community languages and the growing interest in dialect material. It should enhance the quality of access to material through those languages. Similarly, high priority should be given to applications that support the creation of digital content that reflects and enriches the multilingual and multicultural reality of everyday life in the UK.

## 5.7 Support actions


The primary purpose of the New Opportunities Fund is to support the creation of information content in digital form, and the majority of applications will be for digitisation projects. There is, however, a need for a limited number of support actions that will assist the overall effort to create digital content. To meet this need, it will be necessary for the Fund or its agent to commission the work through competitive calls for tender. Such solicited applications are eligible within the terms of reference of the Fund, provided that they can be shown to be the most effective way of achieving the Fund's objectives. The support actions envisaged here will not in themselves produce digital content. Rather, they will overcome technical or other barriers which might otherwise constrain the development of content. The Fund should therefore be prepared to finance a limited number of support actions of this kind, particularly in the initial phase. Priority should be <!-- PAGE: 29 --> given to the support actions that are likely to make the greatest impact on the overall level of content creation. A key support activity will be to establish which technical standards should be adopted for the Network. Without such a common framework there is a danger that material will not be universally accessible. There will also be a need to establish or develop common formats or standard approaches to the structuring of information. The management of intellectual property rights will be critical. The agency should draw up eligibility guidelines on intellectual property for use by applicants. It should also commission the preparation of standard licences for use with commercial publishers and others. There may also be a need for specific training activities over and above those supported through the training strand of the New Library Network initiative. Such training may be required in libraries undertaking ambitious content creation programmes and in organisations that are not covered by the training strand of the New Opportunities Fund. There are some general training courses available. The Fund or its agent should review the relevance of these and, if necessary, should commission the design of training specifically intended to meet the requirements of those creating digital learning materials for the New Library Network. The cost of undertaking such training should be met from the budgets of individual projects. There is scope for research into the underlying issues associated with the creation of digital content, and support should be given to the content theme in the Library and Information Commission's national research strategy. 1 This should be drawn to the attention of the British Library Research and Innovation Centre and others.

## 5.8 Sustainability


Applicants should be able to provide clear evidence that the information content that is created with support from the Fund will be sustained for some years to come. This is not simply a matter of keeping the learning materials available on the Network: where necessary, arrangements should be in place to ensure that the content is kept up to date and is developed in line with changing technology and learning needs.

## 5.9 Commercial alternatives


The involvement of commercial organ isations has been discussed in Chapter 4. There should be a general presumption against funding projects where there is a viable commercial alternative. # 6 Administering the awards


Distributing the £50 million allocated for content creation will be a major task. The New Opportunities Fund needs to ensure that the money is allocated to the applications that are most likely to result in the creation of high-quality content that is relevant to the users of the New Library Network and the National Grid for Learning. The arrangements for administering the awards will therefore be critical to the success of the initiative.

## 6.1 The need for support


The administration of the awards for content creation will involve a number of different processes. These include:

- promoting awareness of the initiative;

- inviting applications and advising applicants;

- assessing the applications;

- selecting those suitable for support;

- administering the payments;

- monitoring;

- reviewing and evaluating the projects and the initiative as a whole.

These functions call for a degree of specialist expertise and an organisational structure that is capable of handling a considerable volume of work. We understand that the New Opportunities Fund wishes to operate as efficiently and effectively as possible in administering the awards. In view of this we recommend that the New Opportunities Fund consider working on a contractual basis with an established agency which will undertake some aspects of the process in support of the administration of the initiative. Final decisions on which applications to fund must rest with the board of the Fund, but some other functions may be delegated. The functions that might not be delegated to the agency are the actual payment of awards and the evaluation of the overall initiative. The New Opportunities Fund will be establishing a grants payment mechanism, and it might be more cost-effective for the Fund to use this mechanism to make the payments. We also feel that it would not be appropriate for the agency that had assisted in administering the scheme to undertake the overall evaluation of the initiative. This might be carried out by a separate independent organisation.

<!-- PAGE: 33 -->

The range of functions which an agency might be contracted to undertake need to be considered alongside the need for similar support which may be required for the training strand and the network implementation.

### 6.1.1 Specialist expertise


Specialist expertise is needed to ensure that the materials produced through this initiative are of the highest possible quality and are relevant to the learning and other needs of the users of the New Library Network. To achieve this, it may be necessary to call on a range of specialist advice. At the strategic level, there is a need for advice on the direction to be taken by the content creation strand to ensure that it remains relevant to the changing needs of the public library service and its users and reflects the diversity of expertise and resources in different sectors. Clearly, matters of policy will be decided by the board of the New Opportunities Fund, but the board should expect to receive informed advice on strategic matters. One option might be for the New Opportunities Fund to contract with an established agency which will undertake to give independent advice in assessing bids which would then be considered by the Fund’s board in consultation with perhaps an expert advisory panel convened for that purpose. It will also be necessary to liaise with other interested parties. The Heritage Lottery Fund is an obvious example, and in section 6.6 we discuss the particular need to co-ordinate activities between the two bodies. But there will also be a wide range of representative and other bodies who should be consulted over various aspects of the operation of the initiative. At an operational level, the case officers employed by the NOF should be able to advise applicants on the operation of the scheme and the eligibility of possible proposals. With a new initiative of this kind, it is important that potential applicants have a clear idea of what is required and are thus able to exploit the full potential of their proposal. The case officers should also be able to sift through applications once they have been received and make the judgements necessary to put them in an order of priority. There is an additional need for specialist advice on individual proposals. Here again, the Fund’s board should be able to call on informed and independent opinion on the suitability and relevance of proposals, on their lifelong learning potential, and on the ability of the applicant to deliver high-quality results. It will also be important to ensure that the proposals conform to the technical eligibility criteria set out in section 4.4. Advice of this kind on individual proposals could be obtained through individual referees or through the use of one or more specialist advisory panels, as discussed in section 6.4.1.

### 6.1.2 The volume of work


There is likely to be a considerable volume of work associated with this initiative. At this stage it is not possible to forecast either the likely number of applications or the likely number of awards. The numbers are, however, expected to be substantial. Each application will need to be processed and assessed, and many will have to be sent to referees. In some cases, as we have noted, it may be thought worthwhile to discuss the formulation of a proposal with an applicant before a formal submission is made, in order to ensure that it meets the requirements of the initiative. This may be time- consuming. There should also be scope for launching calls for proposals on specific topics. Such calls may also be time-consuming, although they may frequently produce significant results. It will also be necessary to initiate the support actions that are needed to maximise the potential benefit from the initiative. Allied to this is the actual distribution of funds. This needs to be handled in a financially secure and accountable manner that accords with public-sector accounting principles.

It could assist with monitoring, review and the evaluation of proposals, making recommendations to the Fund about the proposals that are deemed suitable for funding, but the final decisions on individual awards must rest with the Fund.

## 6.3 Arrangements for regions and the home countries

There should also be a mechanism that formally takes account of the need to represent <!-- PAGE: 35 --> the interests of each of the regions and the four home countries - perhaps, in England, working with the proposed regional cultural forums.

## 6.4 Aspects of administration


The first task will be to establish detailed systems and procedures, so that applications can be dealt with as soon as possible. The NOF will need to specify the subject areas of interest sufficiently clearly for potential applicants to assess the suitability of their plans. It will need to establish procedures and timetables for application. It will need to promote awareness of the initiative and the procedures for application - which might perhaps best be achieved through workshops and open days held throughout the UK. It will also need to produce general guidance for applicants.

### 6.4.1 The application process


Once the applications begin to flow in, the Fund will need to respond. As we have noted, this is a new initiative and it will   be desirable to provide advice to intending applicants before they submit a formal application, so as to minimise the number of ineligible or unsuitable proposals. Given the constraints on resources, it will be necessary to devise cost-effective ways of providing this advice and again, NOF may wish to contract with the established agency to undertake this work, advising potential applicants on the suitability of potential projects. The formal handling of applications then begins. Here it will be necessary to start by extracting ineligible or clearly unsuitable applications. The remainder should be assessed against the established priorities. At this stage it may be possible to weed out a further selection of low-priority proposals, though much will depend on the number of applications received. The proposals deemed eligible and of reasonably high priority should then be sent out to referees. The refereeing process is an important element within the scheme. It provides an opportunity for experts to look closely at individual applications and probe their strengths and weaknesses. If handled properly, it also provides an opportunity for the experts to suggest ways in which an applicant might strengthen or improve a proposal. It also offers an effective means of increasing awareness of the scope and nature of proposals coming forward under the scheme. This last feature will be particularly important in a new initiative such as this. One option would be for the New Opportunities Fund board to contract with an agency to provide independent advice on individual applications; alternatively, it could be supported by one or more specialist subject advisory panels. This offers more flexibility for using a wide range of experts. The panel approach is efficient, in that proposals can be dealt with in batches and panel members will be able to build up a consensus on the <!-- PAGE: 36 --> suitability of applications. The New Opportunities Fund should seek advice from other grant-making bodies, such as those associated with the National Lottery and others such as the Joseph Rowntree Foundation, which each year dispenses a similar amount of money. Having evaluated the applications, with such specialist advice as it sees fit, the New Opportunities Fund board can then make the final decision on which proposals are to receive funding.

### 6.4.2 Managing the distribution of resources


Applicants should not receive their award in a single lump sum. Rather, they should be reimbursed in stages on submission of claims against expenditure that has already occurred. This process needs to be managed quite carefully if accounting and audit requirements are to be satisfied. As payments come to be made it will be necessary to ensure that claims relate to actual expenditure incurred on the project. Further, the New Opportunities Fund will need to make sure that satisfactory progress is being make with the work and that agreed targets and milestones are attained.

- cultural enrichment;

- citizenship and modern government;

- reskilling the nation.

Within these broad areas it might be desirable to focus on a particular issue or event and to launch a call for proposals around it. Here it will be necessary to define the sc ope of the call, promote awareness of it, and handle the applications that result. In such circumstances it might be more appropriate to invite the agency to undertake this work and, perhaps with further support of an expert panel to advise the Fund’s board, to assess the applications within the parameters of the theme in question.

- establishing technical standards;

- developing common formats;

- providing advanced training.

## 6.5 Operational considerations


There are two operational considerations that will affect the op eration of the initiative and the work of the agency:

- managing the requirement for matched funding;

- apportioning the overall resources between competing priorities.

### 6.5.1 Matched funding


All applicants to the New Opportunities Fund should be requir ed to demonstrate their commitment to the project in hand by offering to match the resources made available by the Fund. This is a reasonable requirement, but, as we noted in Chapter 4, it could stifle the flow of applications if applied too rigorously. The New Opportunities Fund therefore needs to apply the matched funding requirement with some care. Both the Fund and the applicants need to be clear about what can be counted towards the matched funding. Content creation is labour-intensive

- particularly when intellectual value is to be added through processing and

the compilation of themed packages. Applicants should therefore be able to count the value of staff time towards their matching funds. The value should be assessed in terms of the full salary cost to the employer. Applicants should also be able to count other production costs towards their contribution. A more difficult issue concerns the value of the intellectual property embedded in the material that will be created. Applicants will clearly be contributing intellectual property that can reasonably be expected to count towards the matched funding: they will be surrendering their right to profit from the use of the property when accessed through the New Library Network. The Scottish Cultural Resources Access Network (SCRAN) has developed an easily comprehensible approach to the valuation of such assets. This uses a unit valuation based on the average cost of digitising assets, such as an image along with accompanying interpretative material. The current valuation stands at £50.50 per asset. When calculating their contribution to matched funding, applicants are able to count half of this amount in compensation for giving the Network a perpetual non-exclusive licence to use the material for educational purposes. Such an approach makes the <!-- PAGE: 39 --> valuation of intellectual property assets very straightforward, and should be adopted by the agency.

### 6.5.2 Apportionment


To ensure an even distribution of resources, the agency should apportion funds to take account of the needs for UK-wide, national, regional and local material, and of the need to cover adequately the three subject areas: cultural enrichment, citizenship and modern government, and reskilling the nation. The potential return on investment is clearly greatest for content that will be relevant throughout the UK. We therefore suggest that about 60 per cent of the resources should be targeted at projects of this kind. About 25 per cent should be directed at projects of home country or regional significance, and about 15 per cent at projects of local focus but national interest. Within this distribution, broadly equal amounts should be spent on creating content within each of the three subject areas. This principle needs to be applied flexibly in order to secure the maximum gain from the range and quality of the projects funded. For example, the third area - reskilling the nation - is likely to contain a greater proportion of material that is equally relevant throughout the UK, as there is less need to take account of local and regional differences. This category may therefore require a slightly smaller allocation. These proportions will vary between subject areas from year to year during the life of the initiative. For example, it will be desirable to fund some big projects initially, because they will probably take longer to come to fruition. It will also be necessary to maintain a balance between funds committed and actual expenditure. The agency should aim to commit the bulk of its funds well before the end of its operating period, to allow for time required to complete the work. Finally, it will be necessary to set aside a small proportion of the resources to finance the support actions.

## 6.6 Co-ordination with the Heritage Lottery Fund


There is a particular need to co-ordinate the work under this initiative with the digital content creation activities of the Heritage Lottery Fund. The Heritage Lottery Fund supports the creation of digital content from all sectors and all types of institution. Its aim is to preserve and to enhance or widen public access, understanding and enjoyment of physical and cultural aspects of the heritage. (Its aims and objectives are described more fully in Appendix 7.) There is therefore potential for overlap between the content <!-- PAGE: 40 --> creation activities of the Heritage Lottery Fund and the New Opportunities Fund's support for creation of digital content for cultural enrichment. We have consulted the Heritage Lottery Fund and have agreed that, to avoid confusion, we will draw a distinction between the work of the two bodies based on the primary purpose for which the material is being digitised. Thus, where the primary purpose of the digitisation is to preserve the cultural heritage, the Heritage Lottery Fund should take the lead. But where the primary purpose is to digitise material so that it can be used for lifelong learning and education in its broadest sense, then the New Opportunities Fund should take the lead. There will inevitably be some projects where the d istinction is unclear, and the two bodies will need to work together to ensure that there is no unnecessary overlap or duplication. They should both be prepared to advise potential applicants on the most appropriate source to approach, and where necessary they should be prepared to refer applications to the other organisation. There may also be scope for joint funding of projects which combine preservation and educational or learning objectives. In such cases, however, the resources provided by one of the funders may not be used as matched funding for the other body. Much of the digital content created through grants from the Heritage Lottery Fund will be suitable for use in the New Library Network. There is therefore a strong case for the adoption of common standards and approaches by both funders, and we recommend that the two organisations should work closely together on the promulgation and application of such standards.

## 6.7 Timescales for the awards


The initiative is due to run for four years, fro m 1999 until the end of 2002. During this period, applications could be dealt with in two different ways. The New Opportunities Fund could simply invite applications from interested parties and deal with them as they come in. Or it could establish a timetable with regular deadlines for submission and then deal with the applications in batches. The first - open - approach has its merits. It enables applicants to proceed at their own pace, and it means that the task of handling applications is spread out over the whole period. We believe, however, that the second approach is preferable. It enables the flow of work to be managed more effectively, because applications can be dealt with in batches, thus offering some economies of scale. Further, the existence of a deadline can provide what is often a much-needed spur to applicants and, as a result, can bring forward the submission of applications. Dealing with applications in <!-- PAGE: 41 --> regular batches will also make it easier for the Fund to monitor the use of its resources and to make any necessary adjustments. We therefore recommend that the New Opportunities Fund should establish quarterly submission deadlines – probably beginning in early to mid 1999, by which time all the guidance to applicants will have been prepared and people will have had time to assemble their proposals and identify sources of matched funding. The New Opportunities Fund or its agent will also have had time to establish its procedures. It will be helpful if the Fund harmonises its timetable with the timetables of other funding bodies. The precise timetable for dealing with applications will need to be established after consultation with other agencies experienced in such operations. We believe, however, that it would be reasonable to expect all applications to be sifted and those eligible refereed within one month of the submission deadline. The results could then be considered and the applications be placed in priority order and submitted to the Fund's board, with recommendations, within the second month from the deadline. Depending on the timing of the board's meetings, decisions could be made and applicants be informed within three months of the deadline. This is, we believe, an ambitious but not unattainable target. If it is thought appropriate to issue themed calls for proposals, these could be fitted into the same timetable. There remains the question of the allocation of awards over the duration of the initiative. Clearly the aim should be to proceed at some speed, to ensure that high-quality content is available as the Network is rolled out. The New Opportunities Fund should therefore expect to commit the bulk of the £50 million in the first half of the period. Given that this is a new initiative, it may take a little time for the flow  of applications to build up, but a substantial proportion - probably 25 per cent

- of the resources should be committed by the end of 1999. During 2000 a

further 50 per cent of the resources should be committed, leaving the remainder for award to smaller projects in the final years. Actual expenditure will, of course, lag behind this, and may well continue beyond the end of 2002. It is also likely that some projects will not be completed until after the end of 2002.

# 7 Delivering the content to users


In addition to choosing between eligible applications and awarding and administering grants, the New Opportunities Fund will need to ensure that all content created under the content creation strand will be reliably and consistently delivered through the New Library Network. This chapter describes the issues that need to be addressed, and recommends an approach to harmonising delivery provision.

## 7.1 Digital content delivery issues


A number of issues need to be considered and resolved before the New Library Network can operate effectively:

- rights management;

- service levels;

- sustainability;

- transparency of access.

### 7.1.1 Rights management


Not all of the content created with New Opportunities Fund support will automatically be in the public domain. There is likely to be a distinction between:

- summary information that is freely available across the Internet;

- educational resources that are available free of charge at the point of use under the control

of public libraries and other licensed outlets;

- value-added materials available at extra cost.

The management of the intellectual property rights in this information should be conducted within the framework developed for the initiative and embodied in the standard licence (see section 5.7). Any content delivery service will therefore need to offer registration, authentication and authorisation facilities to provide appropriate access for potentially millions of users. There may also be a need for image 'watermarking', to protect intellectual property rights and secure encrypted transactions to facilitate per-use payment.

<!-- PAGE: 45 -->

### 7.1.2 Service levels


The images and other multimedia objects that are likely to form a significant proportion of New Opportunities Fund-created content will demand a delivery service with a high- bandwidth network connection of 10 megabits per second between the central library and the ATM node. Between the central library and the branch libraries a bandwidth of 2 megabits per second will suffice in the short term. Depending on the shape of the emerging Network, there may also be a need, for performance reasons, to store copies of popular pages or whole datasets on more than one server, using techniques of caching and mirroring respectively. The demands of learners cannot be restricted to office hours, and the Network will need to be accessible from elsewhere outside the opening hours of libraries. Consequently, any content delivery service must have a scheduled availability of twenty-four hours a day, seven days a week. It must also be able to demonstrate maintenance and disaster-recovery procedures to minimise downtime.

### 7.1.3 Sustainability


It is essential that New Opportunities Fund-created content should be stored in a form appropriate for its long-term preservation and continuing future-proofing as technology advances. This implies digitisation and off-line storage at archival quality, combined with maintenance of a lower-resolution surrogate that can be upgraded to shadow the developing needs and capabilities of the user community. It also implies that any delivery service must be able to demonstrate long-term financial viability, whether through continuing grant aid, top-sliced funding, sale of site licences, or a combination of these and other income streams.

### 7.1.4 Transparency of access


Some New Opportunities Fund-created content will be dynamic and require constant updating. This might be an argument for storing it at the institution responsible for its maintenance. On the other hand, most content - such as digitised images - tends to be static. Once created, it will not need updating and it may best be delivered from centres where sustainability, service levels and rights management facilities can be guaranteed. Whatever the point at which data is stored or delivered, it is essential that the end-user encounters as few barriers as possible to its easy access. Descriptions and access information for the content - known as metadata - should be created in a form that conforms to the standards, best practice and conventions of the domain (library, museum or archive) of its origin. They should also offer cross-domain access and retrieval. This implies consistency between individual New Opportunities Fund content providers, and metadata tailored to the needs of the end-user. The standards embodied in the Dublin Core and IMS are recommended as the standards to be adopted. Search and retrieval protocols for the engines that provide access should be independent of any particular computer or software platform. Here, Z39.50 is recommended as the standard for general accessibility. Gateway services should be supported only if they provide links to services that have been evaluated both for authority of content and for robustness of delivery provision. Digital objects created through the New Opportunities Fund and retrievable through the Network should each be uniquely identified in some way.

<!-- PAGE: 46 -->

Authentication and authorisation facilities should be harmonised so that users do not have to memorise a range of different usernames and passwords depending on the content they wish to access.

## 7.2 Harmonising content delivery


It is recommended that the New Opportunities Fund should draw up and administer a service definition for content delivery, specifying minimum acceptable requirements for each of the issues listed above. Content providers would be invited either to contract to provide a delivery service to these standards or to allow the agency to manage the data on their behalf. In the latter case, contracts for data delivery would be put out to tender. A number of existing services, such as those funded by the Joint Information Systems Committee in the higher education sector - for example, BIDS (the Bath Information and Data Service), MIDAS (the Manchester Information and Data Archive Service) and EDINA (the Edinburgh Information Network Access) - and the Scottish Cultural Resources Access Network for schools, are likely to tender for such contracts, and others may be formed specifically for New Opportunities Fund content delivery. Over time, particular data services may be seen to have strengths in serving particular subject areas, geographical areas, user communities or types of data. However, the agency should work to ensure that content created with New Opportunities Fund support is so delivered as to appear to the user on the New Library Network as part of a coherent whole.

## 7.3 Summary of recommendations on delivering the content to users


- The New Opportunities Fund will need to ensure that all content created under the content

creation strand will be reliably and consistently delivered through the New Library Network (sections 7.1 and 7.2).

- The New Opportunities Fund should ensure that any content delivery service offers

registration, authentication and authorisation facilities and a twenty-four-hour service (sections 7.1.1 and 7.1.2).

- The Network will require a delivery service with a high-bandwidth network connection of

10 megabits per second between the central library and the ATM node. Between the central library and the branch libraries a bandwidth of 2 megabits per second will suffice in the short term (section 7.1.2).

- New Opportunities Fund-created content should be stored in a form appropriate for its

long-term preservation and continuing future-proofing as technology advances (section 7.1.3).

- The content description standards embodied in the Dublin Core and IMS are

recommended as the standards to be adopted. Z39.50 is recommended as the search and retrieval standard (section 7.1.4).

- It is recommended that the agency should draw up and administer a service definition for

content delivery, specifying minimum acceptable requirements on each of the issues listed above (section 7.2).

# 8 Introduction to Part 2


The Library and Information Commission's report New Library: The People's Network sets out a vision of the future. It describes a public library service which plays an integral part in the intellectual life of the community, and emphasises the importance of public library staff both in bringing about the new forms of service and in delivering them. In this section we outline the steps that will  be needed to equip the staff working in public libraries with the skills and abilities that they will need in the library service of the future.

## 8.1 The importance of trained staff


For some, the essence of a public library is its collection of books an d other materials. In reality, though, these assets are of only limited value without staff trained to exploit them. In the future, as digital information services extend the potential range of material far beyond the capacity of any single library building, the staff will be even more important. Library staff will play the central role in shaping the developing use of the public library service. They will need to manage the collections of materials - cataloguing, indexing and retrieving items on demand. They will provide access to the expanding range of digital information available through the New Library Network. They will also need to support people who use the service. They will be the key to the resource. To realise this potential, staff need to be trained. They need to develop new skills and abilities. In particular, they need to become familiar with information and communication technology (ICT) and to feel comfortable both when using it themselves and when helping others to make constructive use of it. They need a range of skills that will enable them to exploit the potential of the technology and so deliver the high-quality services that will be required. Yet an indicative training needs analysis carried out for the Training Task Group showed that there is still a considerable gap between the levels of skill required and those currently available. Further, within library authorities (or boards in Northern Ireland) there are significant disparities between the levels of skill possessed by different groups of staff. Our aim is to overcome these disparities so that everyone using public libraries will be able to deal with staff who are knowledgeable, comfortable in their use of technology, and well able to guide users and help them come to terms both with the technology and with the resources it delivers.

<!-- PAGE: 52 -->

## 8.2 Roles and functions


New Library: The People's Network describes the new roles that public librarians will have to play. Principally, they will need to be able more actively to help library users to learn. Public libraries will need to be an integral part of the future learning society. Public libraries will help people progress through their formal education and training, providing, as they have always done, a source of information and support which complements that provided through the formal system. They will also play an important role in supporting lifelong learning, acting as a means of accessing the increasingly wide range of learning materials available in print and digital form. In particular, they will act as the key to enable people to unlock the digital learning materials that will be made available through the content creation strand of the New Opportunities Fund. More generally, library staff should be able to provide people w ith access to the world's information resources. Here technology is transforming the situation. Where once we had to rely on inter-library lending of books and other materials, we now have the capacity to access and immediately download material that previously was inaccessible. But, as the amount of material that is available has grown, so too has the importance of skilled gatekeepers who can guide users through the systems so that they find what they require. Books will continue to play an important rol e in this digital future, however. Inculcating a reading habit early in life and enriching it through guided access to the wide range of printed materials will continue to be a central part of the work of public librarians. The future will also bring a r apid growth in the number of public services that are delivered remotely through digital networks. The government has ambitious plans to develop electronic government services, and local authorities and other public services, such as the National Health Service, are not far behind. Many people will access these services from their homes, but there will continue to be a group of people who are technologically disenfranchised, through lack of resources or lack of familiarity with the technology. These people should be able to turn to the public library - both to gain access to the services and also to find people who will help them overcome the barriers to using them. All these new roles and functions will call for new skills and abilities on the part of public library staff.

## 8.3 Terms of reference

# 9 The present position


We were required by our terms of reference to undertake an analysis of the need for training in information and communication technology. Given the time constraints, it was not possible to carry out a full needs analysis, but the British Educational Communications and Technology agency (BECTa) carried out an indicative survey on our behalf, the results of which are summarised below. The full report of the study is included as Appendix 9. We thought it important also to review the current pattern of provision of training, and in this we were greatly helped by representatives of library authorities and others who presented evidence at a number of witness sessions. By comparing these two brief surveys it has been possible to identify the gaps which we feel the training supported by the New Opportunities Fund should seek to fill.

## 9.1 The training needs


The BECTa survey provides an overall view of training need. Publ ic library authorities will need to identify their own training needs as they implement the New Library Network locally. However, from the wealth of data provided by the study, clear patterns become apparent.

### 9.1.1 Key findings


A number of key findings emerged from the study:

- Managers of all kinds have typically had significantly more exposure to

information and communication technology than front-line workers.

- The training priorities for front-line workers include basic ICT skills,

navigation of the Internet, and equipment troubleshooting.

- Middle managers' needs relate well to five roles outlined in New Library:

The People's Network:

- net navigator;

- IT gatekeeper;

- information consultant;

- information manager;

- educator.

- The two most popular methods of training across all groups were:

- a short course with continuing online support in the workplace;

<!-- PAGE: 55 -->

- in-house training courses.

- Training routes should be flexible, and should give individuals the

opportunity to obtain qualifications.

- Time, funding, attitudes to information and communication technology,

and limited technology infrastructures emerged as possible barriers to training.

- There were concerns about the difficulties of training part-time staff and

staff who work in single-person service points.

- Concerns emerged about the ability of school librarians to access the

training, and about the risk of their being isolated.

### 9.1.2 Context and methodology


BECTa was commissioned to carry out a study on behalf of the Library and Information Commission's Training Task Group during June 1998. The aim of the study was to determine and report on the diversity of likely ICT training needs for UK public library staff in the light of proposals concerning implementation of the New Library Network. Its findings should be taken as indicative rather than final. The study comprised four interrelated elements: a postal questionnaire, telephone interviews, group interviews and a literature search. A total of 199 public library authorities took part in the study. Five postal questionnaires were sent to each library authority, resulting in 901 responses suitable for inclusion in the analysis. Thirty-five telephone interviews and five group interviews were conducted: two in Wales, covering over two-thirds of Welsh authorities, and one in each of the other UK countries.

### 9.1.3 Levels of ICT awareness and use


Most strategic managers, middle managers and staff training co-ordinators reported high levels of awareness of information and communication technology. Almost all (93 per cent) had access to a computer at work, and over half had one at home. Middle managers reported more regular use of ICT applications than strategic managers . Eighty-six per cent of front-line workers had access to a computer at work, and, again, over half reported that they had access to one at home. They typically reported use of fewer applications than middle managers. About half of the front-line workers reported a high level of ICT awareness - a view which was generally substantiated by data from the telephone survey, where managers expressed their views about the training needs of other staff.

<!-- PAGE: 56 -->

The overall pattern - which was remarkably uniform - was for experience of using information and communication technology to follow closely the pattern of employment: that is to say, in general the study indicated that it was senior staff who had been most exposed to ICT in all its applications. This was particularly true in the case of communications applications such as e-mail and use of the Internet, which are both likely to be very important in establishing an effective New Library Network.

### 9.1.4 Priority groups for training


The sheer number of front-line workers - who make up approximately 80 per cent of public library employees - makes them a clear priority group, especially when the limitations of their exposure to information and communication technology are taken into account. The priority here is to ensure that their basic skills, their ability to navigate the Internet and their troubleshooting abilities with equipment enable them to fulfil their roles effectively. Without a solid foundation on which to develop their skills, they are unlikely to be able to provide high levels of public service. Fortunately, attitudes among this group are very supportive of the need for appropriate training. Equally important to the success of the New Library Network, given their key roles as decision-makers and team leaders, are middle managers. This group exhibited a range of potential training needs in terms of the roles sketched out in New Library: The People's Network:

- net navigator;

- IT gatekeeper;

- information consultant;

- information manager;

- educator.

Naturally, such training can best be carried out against a background of strong personal basic ICT skills. The main priorities for training in new library roles for strategic managers are:

- understanding user's needs for information and communication

technology;

- translating technologies into services for users;

- planning for digitisation;

- marketing new skills in the use of information and communication

technology;

- copyright and legal issues relating to electronic media;

- quality assurance and performance indicators for the use of information

and communication technology;

- the use of technology to deliver training;

- the use of mentors and tutors to support staff development.

The telephone survey provided insights into management perceptions of training needs. The predominant view here was that basic ICT skills, <!-- PAGE: 57 --> Internet navigation skills and ICT gatekeeping skills were priority areas across all staff groups.

### 9.1.5 Other ICT training issues


The two most popular methods of training across all the groups were:

- combination of a short course with continuing online support in the

workplace;

- in-house courses.

It appears that only about two-thirds of public library authorities with service development plans and staff development policies included in them references to information and communication technology. Only one in ten of those interviewed reported that they had any experience in producing or digitising in-house content and products. The most frequently mentioned area for digitisation was local studies; little mention was made of specific plans for digitising products to support educational curricula. Time, funding, attitudes to information and communication technology and limited infrastructures emerged as possible barriers to training. There were also concerns about the difficulties of training part-time staff and staff who work in single-person service points. Different views exist over accreditation. From the evidence concerning current ICT training in public libraries, it is clear that while some staff regard an accredited qualification as important, for others it is of less concern. This suggests that training routes should be flexible and librarians should be given the opportunity to choose accredited routes if they so desire.

### 9.1.6 Additional issues from the home countries meetings


Common concerns emerged from Scotland and Northern Ireland about the need for an appropriate agency to manage funding, validate training, and ensure quality for this training initiative. Colleagues at one of the Welsh meetings highlighted the need for training materials in the Welsh language, and some had concerns that there might be authorities who would not bid for training. The English meeting identified the need for a leader in each authority to ensure that training was authority-wide and corporate. The meeting in North Wales drew attention to the desirability of working together to ensure that all library authorities in Wales adopted common standards. There was a common concern about the ability of school librarians to gain access to the training. There was, however, little consensus about whether <!-- PAGE: 58 --> it was better for them to be trained alongside teachers or with public library staff.

## 9.2 The current pattern of provision


It is important to recognise that the New Opportunities Fund training will not be launched into a vacuum. A considerable amount of training in the use of information and communication technology already takes place, and in developing its strategy the Task Group took account both of the present pattern of provision and of the general lessons that arise (see Appendix 10).

### 9.2.1 The changing climate


There is now a much greater awareness of the need for skills in the use of information and communication technology. New Library: The People's Network is one among a number of reports that emphasise the need for people in all walks of life to have better skills in the use of the technology that is coming to dominate our society. The need for enhanced skills is particularly acute among information workers. Not only is their work affected most, they must also help others to come to terms with the new technologies and their application. As a result, there has been a steady development in the training of such workers. The departments of library and information studies have embedded the relevant technology firmly in their curricula; the continuing professional development programmes have given greater emphasis to ICT skills, and many library services have embarked on ambitious programmes to develop the skills of their staff.

### 9.2.2 The need for a culture change


Underlying this training activity is a clear recognition that there is a need for a culture change in many libraries. The greater use of information and communication technology will transform the nature of the service and the working environment of the staff employed. It will produce new forms of demand and expectation among libraries' users, and will call for a different approach from the staff. Change of the magnitude required cannot be brought about easily, and much of the training has been introduced as part of a broader process of culture change within the organisation - in response to increased levels of demand, for example. There will be a continuing need to manage this process of change.

### 9.2.3 The range of skills


The BECTa survey and the analysis of the training activity that has already taken place within public libraries, coupled with the experience of <!-- PAGE: 59 --> academic libraries, make it possible to identify the range of skills that will be required in the library services of the future. Clearly there is a need to become familiar with the technology concerned - to develop a degree of competence that enables all staff to reach a point where the technology holds no fear for them. Only when staff reach this point can they begin to exploit the full capability of the technology and to provide help and support to others. The ability to troubleshoot and to solve problems with the technology is also important. Perhaps the most significant point to emerge is that the changes in the use of technology enhance the need for a set of skills that is fundamental to library work. Thus the experience of library services that have already done much to develop technology-intensive services is that there is a greater demand for interpersonal and customer-care skills and for skills in information-searching. Understanding the technology alone is not enough: the staff must be able to work with and help the library's users to meet their needs. Closely allied to this is the need for better skills in educating, training and supporting users. There is also a need for improved management skills. The importance of change management is clear, but the introduction of ICT has also increased the need for leadership and strategic management skills.

### 9.2.4 Professional education


The departments of library and information studies have done much over the last twenty years to provide their graduates with skills in information and communication technology. All programmes have some kind of technology course, and in most cases use of ICT is fully integrated into the rest of the curriculum. Many departments have also developed continuing education programmes to enable their graduates to update their skills periodically.

### 9.2.5 Continuing professional development


There is a wide range of training aimed at working information professionals. It is provided by the professional associations - the Library Association and the Institute of Information Scientists - by the departments of library and information studies, by consortia of libraries, and by a number of private-sector providers. Increasingly this continuing professional training is concerned with the use and application of information and communication technology. There is also a growing amount of training aimed at non-professional s taff. Some of this is delivered by the providers of professional training. In other cases local colleges of further education have established training courses aimed at library assistants.

<!-- PAGE: 60 -->

One problem with all this training is that it tends to be concent rated in London and the major conurbations. This can make the training very expensive for many library services.

### 9.2.6 In-house training


Some public libraries have developed their own in-house training programmes with the aim of raising the general level of ICT skills among staff. The Task Group took evidence from a number of authorities that had done this, and it was clear that they had been able to make a significant impact at relatively low cost. One of the key success factors appeared to be the use of local providers of training. In one case the public library had formed close links with local colleges of further education which provided the necessary training. In another, the public library had joined with other local libraries to develop a collaborative training programme.

## 9.3 The gap to be filled


The overall picture that emerges is that there is a general increase in the amount of training available for libraries wanting to increase the ICT skills of their staff. But that general increase is insufficient to meet the volume of demand that will be generated by the introduction of the New Library Network. The pattern of provision is also patchy, with much of the training concentrated in London and the large urban centres. Given the spread of public libraries throughout the UK, this is a major deficiency in the present system.

# 10 The funding model


Before considering the various issues concerned with the award of funds under this initiative, we feel that it will be useful to describe the overall approach that we have adopted.

## 10.1 Constraints on the New Opportunities Fund


In our design of the funding model which we believe the New Opportunities Fund should adopt, we have been conscious of the constraints within which the Fund has to operate. A number of these have a particular bearing on the proposed model. First, the awards have to attract partnership funding. We endorse this principle, and in Chapter 14 we set out the level of matched funding that we believe the Fund should require. Second, the training should be by approved providers. In Chapter 14 we set out a mechanism whereby providers of the basic training can be approved by the Fund or its agent. It is, however, more difficult to give prior approval to the providers of the advanced training that we recommend. The diversity of need is, we believe, such that to give prior approval to all the training providers that might be involved would not be cost-effective. We have therefore recommended an alternative approach which will enable the Fund to ensure that the training is provided to nationally accepted standards of quality. The Fund's board has limited powers to solicit applications which clearly further the underlying objectives of the scheme. We believe that it might be necessary to exercise these powers in order to ensure that there is adequate coverage of basic training provision throughout the UK. This too is discussed in more detail in Chapter 14. The Fund's support is available for training only those staff who are in regular employment with the public library service: it is not available for staff who are employed occasionally for relief work, or who work irregularly.  Clearly, in these circumstances, the Fund would support regularly employed staff such as Saturday assistants and those who work regularly, albeit on a casual employment basis. The NOF have also specified that the training should be limited to operational staff. We have considered what is meant in this context by 'operational', and have determined that there are very few public library staff who do not play an operational role. These are mainly cleaners and <!-- PAGE: 62 --> similar categories of staff. All other staff are operational in the sense that they are concerned with the delivery of services to users, and they should all therefore have the skills needed to exploit the full potential of information and communication technology. An issue arises over the number of staff to be trained. The New Opportunities Fund has been directed to allocate the funds to each of the home countries on a per capita basis, based on the full-time equivalent number of staff. This, we believe, could be inequitable. Public libraries in the United Kingdom employ the equivalent of 27,000 full-time staff. In practice, however, many of these work part-time, and the actual number of people employed is, we believe, approximately 35,000 (there are no nationally available statistics to confirm this figure). Further, the ratio between full-time and part-time staff varies from authority to authority. As it takes just as much effort to train a part-time member of staff as it does to train one that works full time, distribution based on full- time equivalents would disadvantage those authorities which employ a higher than average proportion of part-timers. We recommend, therefore, that the Fund base its allocation to the home countries and to library authorities on the actual number of staff employed, not the full-time equivalent. Applications for funds under this initiative are to be made by public library authorities, not individual libraries. This is not a problem. We believe, however, that in some circumstances it might be desirable for library authorities to join together - once funds were allocated - perhaps under a regional umbrella. This could improve the cost-effectiveness of training through the achievement of economies of scale. It could also help to strengthen joint working and regional arrangements. We recommend, therefore, that the Fund's board should be open to encouraging the development of consortia arrangements

## 10.2 The proposed model: basic competence for all plus advanced


provision All public library authorities will be asked to submit applications for training in information and communication technology and its application. Applications should be based on a thorough analysis of training needs. They should set out the authority's plans for ensuring basic standards of competence in the use and exploitation of the technology , underlying plans for advanced training to meet specific needs. There should be a clear rationale for the plans, linking the training to the library's development plan and to the roll-out of the technology to be installed as part of the New Library Network. Public library authorities will buy the agreed basic training from providers approved by the board or its agent, and will reclaim the agreed costs in <!-- PAGE: 63 --> arrears. Advanced training will be bought from providers who will be identified along with proposals for the training to be delivered and who will operate within agreed quality assurance frameworks.

### 10.2.1 Advanced training


There is a need for training to develop a range of advanced skills. These specialist requirements should reflect both the training needs and the development plans of library authorities. We do not, therefore, feel that it is appropriate for us to specify exactly what the skills should be, although in Chapter 11 we have provided indicative examples. Rather, we feel that it should be for individual library authorities to articulate their needs in their applications to the New Opportunities Fund. Training to meet these advanced skill requirements will be provided by whatever bodies are best suited to meet the needs of the local service, working, in the main, within nationally agreed accreditation and quality assurance frameworks.

### 10.2.2 Basic training


Underlying the advanced skills is a basic level of competence.  The primary need is to ensure that all staff have attained a basic level of familiarity with the technology. All staff should then be competent to use this familiarity to do four things:

- to support learning;

- to provide access to information;

- to promote reader development;

- to provide assisted access to remotely delivered public services.

For its basic standard of familiarity with tech nology, we recommend that the board adopt the standards established for the European Computer Driving Licence. This is discussed in more detail in section 12.2 and Appendix 11. This technological competence will, however, need to be supplemented by additional competences relevant to each of the four tasks referred to above - in short, a substantially enhanced version of the European Computer Driving Licence.   In sections 12.3 to 12.6 we have set out our own standards for these four areas of work. The standards codified in the European Computer Driving Licence plus those set for the four supplementary strands comprise the basic standard of competence that all public library staff should attain. To ensure the attainment of these levels of competence, we recommend the use of approved training providers and testing bodies.

### 10.2.3 Comparison with the training model for teachers

<!-- PAGE: 64 -->

The ICT training model for teachers proposed by the Teacher Training Agency requires the approval of a range of training providers that schools can select from. All training providers, however, must provide training based on the Initial Teacher Training National Curriculum for the use of information and communication technology in subject teaching. The main difference between this and the basic model that we propose is that we specify a range of competences that all public library staff should attain, but leave it to the library authorities to determine how, using approved training providers, they can best attain that degree of competence among all their staff. We believe our approach is more viable for public libraries, as it reflects the very wide disparities in levels of competence between authorities and between different groups of staff. These disparities are identified in the indicative training needs analysis whose results are presented in Appendix 9. Our approach therefore avoids the potential waste of resources whereby all staff would go through a basic training course regardless of their previous ability. Another key element of the teachers' scheme is the prior approval of training providers. Given the large number of teachers involved, this is a viable strategy as the cost of approval is widely spread. However, such prior approval would be less cost-effective when dealing with the smaller number of public library staff. We therefore recommend that, for the technology element of the basic training, the New Opportunities Fund use the prior approval of test and training centres that is built into the arrangements for the European Computer Driving Licence. This approval is undertaken by the British Computer Society, which accredits a wide range of courses in the information technology field. By this means, the board will be able to keep costs low by using an established and quality- assured accreditation mechanism - and, furthermore, one that will be sustained beyond the life of the initiative. It will, of course, be open to organisations such as regional library consortia or other library-related training providers to apply for accreditation. The board or its agent should, however, approve the training provided for the four supplementary strands of the basic training. Under the model we have proposed, there will be no prior accreditation or approval of the providers of advanced training:

- We feel that it is desirable to leave the precise specification of training

needs to individual authorities, which are best placed to balance their service development requirements with their training needs. It would therefore be difficult to give prior approval to potential providers of training that had yet to be specified.

- Even if this were not the case, considerations of cost-effectiveness would

make it hard to justify prior approval. We envisage that probably 80 per <!-- PAGE: 65 --> cent of the fund will be spent on attaining the basic levels of competence, with less than £4 million being spent on the purchase of advanced training. The training needs are diverse, calling for provision from a broad range of providers. Further, the numbers of staff coming forward for any particular type of training will be small. This suggests that the cost of prior approval or accreditation would be high in relation to the number of people to be trained by any single provider. We recognise that this leaves a problem of qu ality assurance. If there is to be no prior accreditation, how is it to be ensured that the training is of the highest quality? To overcome this, we feel it is appropriate to place on applicants a requirement to show in their applications that the training will be delivered by providers that can demonstrate that they are working within nationally accepted quality assurance frameworks. This is discussed in more depth in Chapter 11.

## 10.3 The need for an agency


There is, we believe, a need for an agency to administer the scheme on behalf of the New Opportunities Fund. First and foremost, there is a need for specialist expertise to evaluate applications, to manage the approval process, and to assess the validity of plans for advanced training. There is also a need for liaison with the British Computer Society over the use and development of the European Computer Driving Licence. In Chapter 14 we discuss the potential functions of this agency in more detail.

## 10.4 Summary of recommendations on the funding model


- The Fund should base its allocation to the home countries and to library

authorities on the actual number of staff employed, not the full-time equivalent (section 10.1).

- The Fund's board should be open to encouraging the development of

consortia arrangements between library authorities (section 10.1).

- The training should consist of two components: advanced training and

underlying  basic training in the use and exploitation of information and communication technology (section 10.2).

- The Fund should not attempt to accredit the providers of advanced training

but should require applicants to demonstrate that the training will be delivered by providers working within nationally accepted quality assurance frameworks (section 10.2).

- For the technology element of the basic training, the New Opportunities

Fund should use the prior approval of test and training centres that is built into the arrangements for the European Computer Driving Licence. The board, or its agent should, however, approve the training provided for the four supplementary strands of the basic training (sections 10.2, 10.3).

# 11 Advanced training


Public libraries do not provide a single, homogeneous, basic service: they also offer a combination of specialist services, each designed to meet a different set of community needs. It is therefore important that the basic training described in the next chapter is a pathway to equip key library staff to develop the skills they require to deliver the type of library service envisaged in New Library: the People’s Network, including a range of advanced and specialist requirements. The training initiative should also recognise that the existing level of competence in information and communication technology and its use varies significantly from authority to authority. In some, many if not all of the staff have already attained a level of competence broadly equivalent to the basic standard specified in the next chapter. In such cases it is legitimate for the public library authority to invest the training resources in the development of more advanced skills among the staff. Even in the most poorly provided authority a number of staff will exceed the basic level of competence and, while the bulk of the resources should be used to raise the general level to that of basic competence, there will be value in providing advanced training for those who can best exploit it. It would not be appropriate for the New Opportunities Fund or its agent to be too prescriptive at this stage. Needs and requirements will differ, and, to maximise the benefit from the initiative, it is important that public library authorities are able to design a programme of advanced training that will best meet their needs. When applying for funds under this initiative, each public library authority should specify in its training plan the range and nature of the advanced training that is required. This will be determined by the type of services provided by the library and by its training needs analysis. On the basis of these it should set out a detailed plan for the advanced training, identifying the goals, the standards to be attained, and the providers to be used. To provide some guidance both to the New Opportunities Fund and to public library authorities, we indicate below the type and range of advanced training that we have in mind.

## 11.1 New roles


The aim of the advanced training is to equip key staff in the library service with the skills they will require to develop the type of high-quality service that is envisaged in New Library: The People's Network.

<!-- PAGE: 67 -->

The starting point for the identification of the advanced training requirements is the new roles that public librarians will play in the future. These include:

### 11.1.1 Net navigator


This involves helping people find their  way around the information resources contained on the Internet and on other digital information sources, and more generally exploiting the full potential of the Internet as an information resource.

### 11.1.2 Information technology gatekeeper


This is prima rily concerned with the management and deployment of information and communication technology in the library and with planning its use to develop the service. At the service delivery level, the gatekeepers' role is to provide supported access to ICT in the library and to help people make the most of the technology.

### 11.1.3 Information consultant


Here the librarian is responsible for advising people on the sources of information that are most appropriate to their needs. Librarians need to understand the technology requirements of the users and to be aware of the information and technological resources in appropriate organisations.

### 11.1.4 Information manager


In this role, librarians are responsible for organising and exploiting the information resources of the library. They need to be able to take a strategic view of developments, working with local authority elected members and other organisations and ensuring the technology is used to greatest effect.

### 11.1.5 Educator


This sets all these activities in a learning context. The educator role encompasses the training of both staff and users in their use of information and communication technology and the application of that technology to support a wide range of lifelong learning objectives. All these roles are grounded in an understanding of the technology and an ability to exploit it for the public good. They do, however, represent a significant change of direction for many staff, and there will be a need for advanced training designed both to support these roles and to stimulate a wider process of culture change among library staff.

## 11.2 New skills

<!-- PAGE: 68 -->

The range of advanced skills required by these new roles is considerable. The precise combination will, as we have noted, vary from authority to authority, but might include the following:

### 11.2.1 Net navigator


- In-depth searching skills - the ability to conduct complex searches using

the Internet and other digital information sources. This requires a detailed understanding of different search engines and search strategies, using, for example, Boolean logic. It also requires the ability to identify what are often complex information needs among users.

- Validating Web sites - an important skill is the ability to evaluate both the

source and the content of information retrieved.

- Using alerting services - this is the ability to set up the technology so that

it retrieves information regularly or when a significant change or addition takes place within an information source. In this way librarians are able to provide users with a current-awareness or selective-dissemination-of- information service.

### 11.2.2 Information technology gatekeeper


- Web design skills - the design of effective Web pages and sites is an

advanced skill that the new public library should be able to draw upon.

- Mounting and updating information - once a Web site has been designed,

it is necessary to load information into it, creating all the necessary links and ensuring that the information is kept up to date.

- E-mail databases - public libraries could use e-mail to disseminate

information to identified client groups, such as owners and managers of small businesses. Setting up and managing these databases and bulletin boards requires a particular set of skills.

- Designing specialist interfaces - some groups of users may benefit from

having specially designed interfaces so that the information is more readily accessible to them. Children and elderly people are two groups whose use of information and communication technology might be encouraged by such interfaces. Similarly, visually impaired people can be provided with independent access to a wide range of information not currently available to them.

- Setting up digital links - the technology offers the possibility of

establishing direct links between the library and its users, as well as links to other sources of information. Setting up these links so that they are easy to use, reliable and secure requires a particular set of skills.

### 11.2.3 Information consultant

<!-- PAGE: 69 -->

- Analysis and diagnosis - the starting point for any information consultancy

is a thorough understanding of the user's information needs. It will therefore be important to develop skills in the analysis and diagnosis of information requirements, so that appropriate use can be made of ICT in the satisfaction of these needs.

- Information sources - if librarians are to be able to advise users on the

most appropriate sources, they must themselves be aware of a wide range of different sources, and their strengths and limitations.

- Partnership - the technology offers greatly expanded opportunities to

develop partnerships with other information providers, and in this way to extend the range of resources available to the library users.

- Information design and presentation - information consultants may be

called upon to present the results of searches conducted with, or on behalf of, the users. To do this effectively they will need to be able to use the technology to design and present information in the most effective ways.

### 11.2.4 Information manager


- Strategic planning - the ability to plan the strategic development of the

library's ICT resources will be a key skill in the new library. Information managers will need to be able to work with strategic planners and elected members in the library authority.

- Legislative and regulatory requirements - the library must ensure that it

does not contravene any legislative or regulatory requirements. It must manage its intellectual property assets and ensure that it conforms to data- protection law.

- Content creation - libraries will become much more closely involved with

the creation of digital content. As well as skills in the design of the information materials, this will call for a range of skills in:

- metadata;

- digital archiving;

- the management of digital collections;

- rights management.

- Organising information for access - the new technology will open up new

possibilities for access to collections of printed material. Skills will be required to ensure that this potential is fully exploited.

### 11.2.5 Educator


- Training and coaching skills - librarians will need to be able to train staff,

and possibly groups of users, in the use of the technology. They will also <!-- PAGE: 70 --> need to be able to work constructively with individuals to coach them in their use of the technology. They should also be able to work with both individuals and groups to help them use the technology as a means of achieving their lifelong learning objectives.

- Designing learning materials and programmes - library staff should be

able to design training and learning programmes, and to support these with effective materials.

- Computer-based training - staff should be familiar with computer-based

training programmes, their strengths and weaknesses. They should be able to identify when their use is appropriate, and should be able to support people in their use of them. Normally, the staff chosen for advanced training will be those who have already met or exceeded the basic level of competence in the use and exploitation of information and communication technology. In some cases, however, it may be appropriate to develop advanced skills before the full package of basic skills has been obtained. This latter situation may arise with staff who are working in parts of a library authority which require the use of advanced skills, and who will therefore be in a position to apply the skills as and when they are acquired. There should be an explicit expectation that, once the staff have acquired the skills, they will exercise them on behalf of the authority. The level of competence to be attained will depend on the number of staff to be trained, their prior level of competence and the demands that will be placed upon them by the library authority.

## 11.3 Meeting specific users' needs


Implicit in the goals of New Library: The People's Network is the desire to make the public library service as relevant as possible to the needs of all sections of the community. The development of advanced skills therefore requires an understanding of the users' needs allied to an appreciation of the ways in which the service can be developed so that it responds to those needs. The goal should be to enable staff to apply and exploit their advanced skills and the technology to meet the needs of particular client groups. There are many groups who stand to benefit from specifically designed services. Disabled people, for example, have many information needs that could be satisfied through the application of information and communication technology, and some public libraries have introduced adaptive ICT to assist independent use. Here the library staff would need to appreciate both the range of needs and the possibilities offered by the technology. On the other hand, a group like elderly people may benefit <!-- PAGE: 71 --> from programmes designed to make them more familiar with ICT and its use. Here the library staff would need to know how best to overcome a reluctance to use the equipment and how to support and encourage use. Another possibility might be to develop technology-intensive information services aimed at meeting the information needs of people running small businesses.

## 11.4 Common characteristics


These advanced training requirements have a number of common characteristics that should inform both the design of training programmes and their evaluation by the New Opportunities Fund or its agent. First, there should be a clear and demonstrable difference between the level of competence to be attained through the advanced training and the competence specified in the basic standards. Not only should the advanced training be more tightly focused, it should aim to develop a markedly higher level of competence. Second, the training should be clearly related to service needs. These might be recognised deficiencies or weaknesses in the present service, or clear plans for future development. The advanced training should not take place in a vacuum, but should be grounded in service development plans. Closely related to this point is the need for the staff receiv ing the training to have ready opportunities to exercise the skills they develop. Implementation of training should therefore coincide with the development of the service. Further, the trained staff should be recognised within the service as having additional skills and expertise, so that other staff are able to draw on these.

## 11.5 Assuring quality


Running throughout the provision of advanced training is the need to build in quality assurance mechanisms. The diversity of need and the differences in the level of training required make it almost impossible for the New Opportunities Fund or its agent to specify quality standards in advance through an accreditation process. The cost of so doing would be disproportionate to the added value that would result. It is, however, essential to ensure that the Fund's resources are used to purchase training that is of the highest possible quality. To achieve this, public library authorities should be required to demonstrate in their applications that the training will be of an assured quality. They can do this in a number of ways. The most satisfactory approach will be to purchase training from providers that are accredited by nationally reputable organisations. Higher and further education institutions in England, for example, are accredited by <!-- PAGE: 72 --> the Higher and Further Education Funding Council for England, and similar arrangements apply in the other home countries. We recommend that the New Opportunities Fund should accept the validity of accreditation provided in this way. In many cases, providers in this sector are also accredited by professional associations. The Library Association and the Institute of Information Scientists, for example, accredit the providers of qualifications in librarianship and information science, and similar arrangements exist for approval and accreditation of information technology courses. Again, we recommend that the New Opportunities Fund should accept the validity of this accreditation. An alternative approach would be to use all or part of a course that aims to achieve a nationally recognised qualification. This might be a module in a degree course or one leading to a Scottish or National Vocational Qualification. The New Opportunities Fund should accept that such courses are subject to the quality controls imposed by the providers of those qualifications. This leaves a small category of training that will not come within any of the above quality assurance mechanisms. It will probably not amount to more than 2 or 3 per cent of the total expenditure under the initiative. Principally it is concerned with tailor-made training courses provided by commercial organisations or regional consortia of libraries. These providers will for the most part operate outside the framework of nationally recognised qualifications and accreditation procedures - not by choice, but because there is simply no procedure within which they could operate. Some of the training provided in this way is of very high quality and could make a real contribution to the development of specialist skills among public librarians. Some, but not all, of these training providers - notably those operating in the commercial sector - have adopted internationally recognised quality standards: ISO 9001 and BS 5750. These provide an assurance that the training will be delivered to established standards following quality- controlled procedures. They do not alone, however, provide the kind of guarantees that the New Opportunities Fund might reasonably expect. Here we believe that there is a case for the Fund to adopt a slightly different approach to quality assurance. Instead of relying on prior accreditation or acceptance of nationally recognised qualifications, the Fund should permit public library authorities to make a case for using training providers operating outside these frameworks. Those cases should then be tested through a refereeing process, the results of which will be taken into account in the recommendation made to the Fund.

To provide guidance both for the board of the New Opportunities Fund and for public library authorities, we have prepared the following example of the way in which a public library might, in its overall application to the Fund, specify its requirement for an advanced training course in the design of screen-based information.

# 1 The need


The development plan for Midshires Public Library, approved by the council on 30 March 1999, sets out an ambitious programme to develop a range of digital information content that can be used within the county and by others as part of the emerging New Library Network. Part of this digital content creation programme will, we hope, be supported by the New Opportunities Fund through its approval of our application under the content creation initiative. This will, however, be supplemented by additional content creation activity directed towards the needs of the local business community and supported by the Midshires Chamber of Commerce. Our plans are to begin this content creation work in the summer of 1 999, so that material will become available when we roll out the full network in the first quarter of 2000. In developing our proposals, we have identified a particular need for skills in the design of information that will be presented on screens, as distinct from information printed on paper. We are conscious that the skills required and the design parameters are different, and this view has been confirmed by the Information Design Association, from which we have sought expert advice. The lack of such skills has been confirmed by a training needs analysis undertaken earlier this year, the results of which are appended. We wish to train three staff so that they have this particular set of advanced skills. It would not, however, be cost-effective to commission special training for such a small group, so we have approached our three neighbouring counties, and together we can assemble a group of ten staff who need such training.

# 2 Aims and objectives


The overall aim of the training is to develop a high  level of skill in the design of screen-based information. Within this broad aim, we have identified three specific objectives:

- to develop an understanding of the broad principles of information design;

- to apply these principles to digital information and screen-based

environments; <!-- PAGE: 74 --> - to develop the practical skills needed to develop effective information

materials for use in a public library context. The training therefore includes both the development of an understanding of underlying principles and the practical application of those principles in a public library context.

# 3 Delivery


The training will be delivered through a four-day training course supplemented by an extended period of two months during which the course presenters will act as mentors and advisers to help overcome problems that arise. The outline course content is as follows:

- Day one

- Basic principles of information design and their

application to digital information

- Day two

Overcoming the constraints imposed by the scre en The use of images and colour The use of hypertext links

- Day three

Advanced planning and overall design Practical sessions Review and feedback

- Day four

Structuring the content Laying out the screens Practical sessions Review and feedback

# 4 Outcomes


By the end of the course the participants should:

- be able to outline the main principles of information design and to show

- be able to identify the opportunities that screen-based information offers

for overcoming design problems;

- be able to plan and design the overall structure of a digital information

product;

- be able to design effective screen layouts and hypertext links, and to make

the most of the use of images and colour; <!-- PAGE: 75 --> - feel sufficiently confident in their skills and abilities to begin work on a

substantive digital information design project.

# 5 The providers


The course will be provided by the Information Design Department which is part of the School of Design at Midshires University. The department offers a range of full-time and part-time courses leading to BA and MA qualifications in information design. In recent years the department has become acknowledged as one of the leading institutions in the field of digital information design, and it has strong links with industry through its consultancy and research programme. The department has recently been awarded a major Economic and Social Research Council grant for research into the underlying principles of electronic information design and its impact on information users.

# 6 Quality assurance


Midshires University is fully accredited by the Higher and Further Education Funding Council for England, and the department received an 'excellent' rating in the last teaching-quality exercise. The training course will be adapted from a more generally available course which is in turn approved by the Information Design Association under its continuing professional development scheme. The adaptation mainly concerns work to ensure that the course is made relevant to the public library context.

- Public library authorities should be required to demonstrate in their

# 12 Basic competences


The basic training provides a foundation that will enable all staff to support people's use of the new public library service. In Chapter 8 of this report we identified four principal roles and functions that all public library staff must be able to fulfil. They must be able:

- to support learning;

- to provide access to information;

- to promote reader development;

- to provide assisted access to remotely delivered public services.

More specifically, library staff must be able to use information and communication technology and the New Library Network to perform these functions. The training supported by the New Opportunities Fund will ensure that all public library staff have the level of competence needed.

## 12.1 The aim


Before they can do anything else, all library staff must be sufficiently familiar with information and communication technology to be able to use it easily and effectively. Most important of all, they should be able to help others use it creatively. The primary aim of the basic training, therefore, must be to overcome barriers to the use of ICT and to promote familiarity and confidence in its use. That alone will not be enough, however. Public library staff must be able:

- to use and exploit the technology to support people's learning;

- to provide them with improved access to information;

- to develop their reading capacity;

- to help them use remotely delivered public services.

We therefore propose that the basic level of competence which should be attained by all public library staff should consist of a foundation in information and communication technology, supplemented by competence in these four functional areas. The current level of ability varies considerably from authority to authority, and some have already devoted considerable effort to the development of these core competences. Even within a single library system, the training needs analysis has shown that the range and level of skills may differ <!-- PAGE: 77 --> significantly between members of staff. We therefore feel it is not appropriate to specify a package of basic training that everyone should undergo. This would be to waste resources, and for many staff it could be counter-productive. Instead, we have specified the level of competence that we believe all staff should attain, and have indicated how they can demonstrate that they are capable of doing what is required of them. The specification will provide a benchmark against which the existing pattern of skills can be measured. It will also enable training providers to design training products.

## 12.2 The foundation


The foundation for the basic leve l of competence is an understanding of the nature of information and communication technology, allied to practical skills in its use. To provide this basic level of understanding and skill, we recommend that all public library staff be expected to attain the standards set out in the European Computer Driving Licence. The European Computer Driving Licence was established last year to provide a Europe-wide qualification that enables people to demonstrate their competence in computer skills. (More information about the licence is given in Appendix 11.) The overall aim of the licence is to encourage a general improvement in the basic knowledge of computers and a higher level of competence in the use of information and communication technology throughout Europe. Within this overall aim there are four specific objectives:

- to raise the general level of competence in information technology;

- to improve productivity at work;

- to enable employers to invest more efficiently in information technology;

- to ensure that best-practice and quality issues are understood and

implemented. To obtain the licence, people have to pass a series of tests at one of a number of approved test centres. The tests cover seven related modules:

- basic concepts of information technology;

- using the computer and managing files;

- word processing;

- spreadsheets;

- databases and filing systems;

- presentation and drawing;

- information network services.

Together these have been designed to cover the key concepts of computing, its practical application, and its use at work and more generally in society. The full syllabus is provided in Appendix 11.

<!-- PAGE: 78 -->

Each individual can judge how much training and practice in each module they require before being tested - just like with the conventional driving test. Training to prepare people to the required standard is available from a growing number of colleges and training institutions, and from many of the approved test centres around the country. In the UK the scheme is administered by the Brit ish Computer Society, working with NCC Education Services Ltd. It was launched in May 1998, and within three months seventy test centres had been accredited; many more are expected to be accredited in the coming year. Each test centre receives an initial approval and it is then audited periodically to ensure that consistent standards are maintained. Test centres are encouraged to offer training to prepare people for the test, although it is also open to others to provide such training.

### 12.2.1 Equivalent standards


The British Computer Society has taken steps to ensure that the standard embodied in the European Computer Driving Licence ties in with other nationally approved qualifications, principally National Vocation Qualifications. The European Computer Driving Licence is broadly equivalent in standard to level 2 of the Scottish or National Vocational Qualification (S/NVQ) scheme, and it covers more than 50 per cent of the syllabus for the level-2 S/NVQ in Using Information Technology. This means that anyone holding the level-2 S/NVQ in Using Information Technology will be deemed to have reached the standard for the European Computer Driving Licence provided that they have completed optional units 2.06, 2.07 and 2.08. Similarly, anyone holding the European Computer Driving Licence is well on the way towards attaining the competence required by the Scottish or National Vocational Qualification. The European Computer Driving Licence standard also covers much of the ground that is covered in the ICT element of library and information courses approved by the two professional associations: the Library Association and the Institute of Information Scientists. These associations could verify which courses attain the required standard and could define a cut-off date before which graduates are unlikely to have reached this standard. The European Computer Driving Licence will provide a common standard of competence in the use of information and communication technology for all public library staff. It will give staff sufficient confidence to be able to sort out problems and difficulties that users have with ICT equipment. The added advantage of using a generally applicable scheme such as this is that public library staff will be very well placed to help others working towards their own Computer Driving Licence.

<!-- PAGE: 79 -->

The licence is not, however, specific to public library applications. It will therefore need to be complemented by skills in using the basic competence in ICT to support learning, to provide access to information, to support reader development, and to help people to use remotely delivered public services.

## 12.3 Supporting learning


Public libraries have a long tradition of helping people to learn, and this role will become more important in the future. Here what is required is an underpinning knowledge of the education system and the nature of learning outside the formal system, allied to an awareness of sources of information and support material.

### 12.3.1 Supporting self-directed and informal learning


To support learners outside the formal education system, all public library staff should understand:

- the difference between formal education, informal learning and self-

directed learning;

- the importance of pre-school learning.

They should be able:

- to identify people's learning needs and to judge the level of material

required to support the learning;

- to find and obtain information materials that will support learning of all

kinds;

- to help parents of young children who are learning to read and learning to

use information materials;

- to direct learners of all kinds to sources of specialist help and assistance.

All staff should be aware of the ways in which information and communication technology can help to meet special learning needs and to assist the learning of visually impaired people.

### 12.3.2 Understanding the education system


All public library staff should have a clear understanding of the structure of the education system. They should understand:

- the nature of local provision and the main local education policies;

- the distinction between primary, secondary, further, higher and adult

education;

- the role of homework clubs and centres and the local pattern of provision;

- the nature of assessment measures of performance, so that they can help

students and parents make informed choices about education;

- the nature of formal qualifications.

<!-- PAGE: 80 -->

They should also be able:

- to find information about local schools and their performance;

- to find information on locally provided further and adult education and the

overall pattern of higher education provision;

- to point people to specialist sources of information and advice within the

local authority and elsewhere;

- to use a range of sources of digital information about education provision,

including the National Grid for Learning. Many elements of the formal education system are undergoing change at present, and it will be important to ensure that public library staff are aware of these changes and their significance.

## 12.4 Providing access to information


The second important function of public library staff is to provide people with access to a wide range of sources of information. For the purposes of this training we must focus on access to electronic information sources, although it should be recognised that there is a continuing need for training in print-based sources. The main source of electronic information in public libraries will be the Internet, and it is on this that attention should focus - not least because many of the principles of searching for information on the Internet can be applied to other electronic information sources. Public library staff should, however, be familiar with CD-roms and online information services where these are available in the library. In particular, they should be able to select the most appropriate source for different purposes.

### 12.4.1 Exploiting the Internet


All public library staff should understand the basic structure and nature of the World Wide Web element of the Internet and should be able to use it to find information. They should also be sufficiently familiar with it that they can help others to use it effectively. This means that they should:

- understand the basic principles of the World Wide Web, including

addresses and domain names, URLs and hypertext links;

- be able to use at least one browser, including features such as bookmarks

and the main functions on the toolbar;

- be familiar with two or more search engines, and be able to construct

advanced searches;

- be able to print information from the World Wide Web;

- be able to download files, save them, and print from them if required.

### 12.4.2 CD-roms and online information

<!-- PAGE: 81 -->

In some libraries, people will also be able to use CD-roms and gain access to online databases. In such circumstances library staff should be able to use these sources to carry out searches and to retrieve information. They should be sufficiently familiar with the sources to be able to help others use them effectively. This means that they should:

- be able to load CD-roms and carry out searches on them;

- be able to go online to remote databases and be able to conduct and refine

searches on specific topics.

### 12.4.3 Exploiting e-mail


Electronic mail - e-mail - offers a range of possibilities for those seek ing information. Bulletin boards, discussion lists, listservs and news groups can all be exploited as information sources. There are, in particular, a growing number of services which are aimed specifically at meeting the needs of the library community. Public library staff should:

- understand the strengths and weaknesses of these different facilities;

- be able to use appropriate services to obtain information on behalf of

library users. Following initial discussions with the British Computer Society, we have established that anyone able to demonstrate the above range of information access skills might be able to claim exemption from module 7 of the European Computer Driving Licence, which covers information network services. Further discussion would, however, be needed to cement this agreement.

## 12.5 Reader development


As we noted in Chapter 8, books will continue to play a central role in our life and culture, and book lending is the most widely acknowledged role of public libraries. Developing reading skill through the use of books is a key step towards the development of functional literacy, and in turn leads on to access to a wide range of educational, cultural and developmental material. In their daily work, public library staff act as reading promoters and guides. They try to stimulate interest in reading by means ranging from the encouragement of reading at the earliest age to the creation of informed reading choice through book displays, promotions and other activities. There will be a continuing need for staff to offer both text-based and networked guides to readers of all ages. If anything, this need will grow through the implementation of the national literacy strategy and the National Year of Reading. Public library staff will need to be familiar with the range of reader services and information available digitally. They should feel comfortable using electronic sources, and be able to help others use them effectively.

<!-- PAGE: 82 -->

This implies both an awareness of reader-related sites and networks that are currently available and the familiarity and skill required to help others use them. All public library staff should, therefore:

- be aware of reader-related sites on the Internet, including:

- thematic approaches and guides to further reading,

- sites on specific genres and authors,

- online bookstores,

- publishers' sites,

- reading groups;

- know about literacy initiatives in education, such as the national literacy

strategy and literacy hour;

- know about Bookstart and other early-reading initiatives, and have a

working knowledge of sites for children and their reading, such as Stories on the Web;

- know about sites and sources of interest to readers with a range of special

needs.

## 12.6 Helping people access public services


In the coming years, more and more public services will be made available electronically. People will be able to interact with central and local government using digital networks to obtain information and to transact business. The public library will play an important role in ensuring that the technologically disenfranchised are not excluded from this form of provision. Public library staff will need to be familiar with the range of services that are available digitally; they should feel comfortable using those services, and should be able to help others to use them effectively. This implies a general understanding of the nature of the development of electronic public services, awareness of the services that are currently available, and the familiarity and skill needed to help others use them. All public library staff should therefore:

- understand the nature of the provision of electronic public services, and in

particular the distinction between information and transaction services;

- be aware of the services that are availa ble through the public library, and

be familiar with the ways in which the services operate;

- be able to work with people who are unfamiliar with digital service

delivery, helping them to obtain the service they require and developing the skills they will need to use the service unassisted.

## 12.7 The package of basic skills


The package of basic skills that we have specified is an ambitious one. It consists of a core of technology skills supplemented by specific skills that <!-- PAGE: 83 --> will enable public library staff to support learning, to provide access to information, to support reader development, and to help people to use remotely delivered public services. We believe that these provide the baseline services that all staff in the public library service should be able to offer.

## 12.8 Summary of recommendations on basic competences


- The basic level of competence which should be attained by all public

library staff should consist of a foundation in information and communication technology, supplemented by competence in four functional areas:

- supporting learning;

- providing access to information;

- supporting reader development;

- providing assisted access to remotely delivered public services (section

12.1).

- To provide the basic level of understanding and s kill in the use of

information and communication technology, we recommend that all public library staff be expected to attain the standards set out in the European Computer Driving Licence. We have specified the standards to be attained in the four complementary functional areas (sections 12.2-12.6).

# 13 School librarians and school library


services The diversity of staffing in school libraries and resource centres has led to a number of discussions about the appropriate route for training in information and communication technology for school library staff. Staff working in school libraries come to do so through a variety of routes. Some are appointed from the teaching force of the school, as holders of posts of additional responsibility. Some are appointed as information professionals with qualifications as chartered librarians or with equivalent information experience. Some are appointed with a mainly administrative or other background, without a qualification in teaching or librarianship. Still others are appointed with a dual-qualification as both teacher and librarian. Just as the New Opportunities Fund training strand applies to all public library staff, so too should it apply to all staff - professional, managerial and support - who are active in the operation of the school library or resource centre.

## 13.1 School librarians


Schools will be required to present a detailed training plan for staff eligible for training under the New Opportunities Fund. While the background of staff working in school libraries may vary, we recommend that such staff should be expected to attain the same standard of competence as is expected of teachers. This is set out in the specification developed by the British Educational Communications and Technology agency. (A more detailed description of the standard to be attained is given at the end of Appendix 9.) There are two possible ways forward to ensure that staff working in school libraries have access to the appropriate training to increase their level of confidence and expertise in the use of information and communication technology:

- Where school librarians are primarily teachers, they should receive the

training specified by BECTa for school librarians.

- As an alternative, where school library staff are prim arily information

workers - qualified librarians or staff with non-teaching backgrounds and experience - they should have the opportunity to follow the public library <!-- PAGE: 85 --> training route, with additional training appropriate to their school-based role. All this training, however, will have to be funded out of the school's New Opportunities Fund allocation for teachers. This choice and diversity should be possible while using the funding allocated to schools under the New Opportunities Fund scheme. We understand that the New Opportunities Fund can only support training for teachers that is supplied by approved training providers. The training devised for public libraries will come from providers approved under that scheme, and we hope that this approval will suffice. Training programmes should be offered both locally and nationally, and the specific training required for school library staff will need to be included as a stand-alone or integrated part of the teachers' ICT training specification to be met by approved training providers. It will be for schools locally to decide which staff receive training, using the total school entitlement, since no special provision has been made for this relatively small number of staff - the Library Association estimates that there are 3,000 such staff throughout the UK. The number of staff involved in any school will be low, and the head teacher will need to assign priorities to the training of both teachers and school librarians based on identification of their individual needs. Access to training for teachers and school library staff will therefore be determined by the level of their training needs and the school's ICT development plan. It should, however, be recognised that the importance of training school library staff was explicitly acknowledged in the Secretary of State's directions to the New Opportunities Fund.

## 13.2 School library services


Across the UK, school library services exist to:

- advise schools and their teaching staff on the appropriate use of resour ces

to support learning;

- support and back up the work of school library staff within schools, often

providing valuable in-service training to maximise the impact of learning- support materials;

- directly loan material to schools for use by teachers.

Most school library services are operated on an agency basis for the local education authority by the public library service. Some are either independent or managed by the education authority. In total there are approximately 1,800 full-time-equivalent staff working in school library services of all kinds across the UK. Where school library services form part of public library provision through the local authority, school library staff will be counted among the total <!-- PAGE: 86 --> number of public library staff and will have the same access to the New Opportunities Fund training as other public library staff. We estimate that 1,600 are in this position. Some school library services have, however, been set up as separate cost centres outside the public library service and are dependent on schools to buy back their services, using devolved capitation allowances. In strict terms these services are not part of the public library authority, despite serving the school community directly and in the same way as colleagues in other school library services. In addition, there are a number of school library services that are operated by the local education authority and so also fall outside the scope of the public library service. Altogether, we estimate that there are 200 people who come into these categories. We strongly recommend that these personnel deserve to be included in the training financed by the Fund. To ensure that school library service staff are not excluded from the programme, we suggest that the New Opportunities Fund should consider two options:

- allow public library authorities to include staff employed in devolved or

local-education-authority-run services in their plans for training, and make the authorities accountable to the Fund for the administration and quality assurance of this training;

- identify a single UK agency to collect and authenticate the submissions of

devolved and education-authority-run services and to make a block request to the Fund on their behalf. The first option extends an existing mec hanism rather than creating a new one and is our recommended option. We believe it is workable and effective, and that it avoids additional administrative costs on behalf of a relatively small number of personnel. There are two ways to ensure that staff working in school library services have access to the appropriate training to increase their level of confidence and expertise in the use of information and communication technology:

- school library service staff may opt to train alongside school library

colleagues, following the route of the teacher training supported by the Fund;

- as an alternative, school library service staff should have the opportunity to

follow the public library training, with additional training appropriate to their role. In the light of this, it may be necessary to review the Secretary of State's direction to the New Opportunities Fund, to ensure that the appropriate pathways to training remain open both to school librarians and to school library service staff.

# 14 Administering the awards


Distributing £20 million to over 200 public library authorities in order to meet a wide range of training needs will be a complex task. To do so while meeting assured quality expectations and to a timetable that will, to a great extent, be determined by the roll-out of the New Library Network will not be easy. In this chapter we try to identify the tasks that will need to be undertaken.

## 14.1 Specialist expertise


In Chapter 10 we concluded that there was a need for an agency to administer the training awards on behalf of the New Opportunities Fund. We are aware that the Fund's board wishes to keep its central staff numbers small and that it intends to contract out a range of operational services to specialist agencies. We feel that this is entirely appropriate in relation to the training strand. The main reason for this is that we believe that the administration of this strand will call for a degree of specialist expertise that will not necessarily be found among the more generalist staff of the Fund. The basis of our model is the submission of training plans by library authorities, and these will need to be assessed to ensure that they offer value for money and are likely to result in high-quality training. The plans for advanced training, in particular, will require close scrutiny by people with sufficient expertise to be able to judge the viability of the proposals. It would be possible to use external referees to make these judgements, but we feel that this would probably be time-consuming and could place an undue burden on people who are already very busy. It would be better, we feel, for the agency to make most of the assessments internally, referring only particularly complex or contentious applications to outside referees. Staff of the agency should also be in a position to evaluate applications from potential training providers. Again there may be some need for external refereeing, but this should complement internal assessment rather than be a substitute for it. There will also be a considerable amount of work involved with the administration of the initiative. Once again, given the Fund's desire to retain only a small staff, this suggests delegation to an agency.

## 14.2 Approving providers


One of the first tasks of the agency should be to approve the providers of the basic training. A key element in our model is the use of the European Computer Driving Licence. The tests for this licence are conducted by a network of test centres that are approved by the British Computer Society, which has an accreditation and review mechanism to ensure that centres conform to its standards. Most of these centres also offer training in preparation for the test and, where this is so, the British Computer Society reviews and approves the training provided. Rather than establish a separate approval mechanism, we recommend that the Fund accept the centres approved by the British Computer Society.

<!-- PAGE: 89 -->

It will, however, be necessary to approve the providers of the other elements of basic training - supplementary training in:

- supporting learning;

- providing access to information;

- promoting reader development;

- providing assisted access to remotely delivered public services.

The agency will need to draw up detailed specifications for the training to be provided, specifying the scope of the training and the outcomes to be achieved. It should not, however, prescribe the means of training; rather, it should seek to encourage providers from both the public and the private sector to come forward with novel and cost-effective solutions. These specifications can form the basis of a c all for proposals from providers. Once the proposals have been received they can be evaluated, and those providers offering good value for money should be approved for use by public libraries under the initiative. The aim should be to have full geographical coverage throughout the UK, with, ideally, each library authority having a choice of provider. If it is not possible to achieve full geographic coverage, or if the board feels that insufficient providers have come forward to ensure a degree of choice for library authorities, it may be necessary to use the Fund's limited powers to solicit applications. It may be the case that new providers emerge during the course of the initiative. It would not be desirable to prevent good providers from becoming part of the scheme. The Fund should therefore reserve the right to announce a second call for applications from providers if demand appears to warrant it.

## 14.3 Allocations to library authorities


The basic principle of allocation is to be the number of staff  employed. Thus the allocations to each of the home countries will be based on the number of staff employed in each country. The same principle should apply at the level of library authorities. This does, however, raise the question of how the staff are to be counted. Much of the discussion has been based on there being the equivalent of 27,000 full-time staff employed in public libraries in the UK. This, however, conceals the fact that many of those staff are employed part-time, and that the distribution of part-timers is not even across the country. Unfortunately, statistics on the numbers of staff actually employed are not available nationally. Limited research that we have undertaken, however, suggests that the 27,000 full-time equivalents correspond to about 35,000 actual employees. Given that it takes just as much effort to train a part-timer as it does to train someone working full-time, an allocation of funds based on full-time equivalents would be inequitable. We recommend, therefore, that the New Opportunities Fund base its distribution of funds on the actual number of staff employed. Clearly it will be for the agency to establish what that number is.

## 14.4 Applications from library authorities


Having approved the providers of basic training and established the basic allocation per member of staff, the agency can call for applications from public library authorities. This call will, however, need to be supported by a programme of awareness-raising and information, to ensure that everyone concerned is fully aware of the operational requirements.

<!-- PAGE: 90 -->

### 14.4.1 Consortia


It is likely that some groups of public library authorities may wish to train  jointly, in order to realise economies of scale and as a means of strengthening the emerging regional collaborative efforts to roll out the New Library Network. Consortia may be formed following the allocation of funds  to carry out a joint training programme. It is also possible that library consortia will apply to become training providers - and to the British Computer Society to become test centres for the European Computer Driving Licence. We believe that the Fund should welcome and encourage such approaches.

<!-- PAGE: 91 -->

### 14.4.2 Training needs analysis


The starting point for an authority's application to the Fund should be a thorough analysis of the need for training in information and communication technology and its exploitation. The basis for such needs analysis exists in the survey conducted for the Task Group by the British Educational Communications and Technology agency. That, however, was, designed as a national tool, and was less specific than an individual library authority would require. We believe that there would be merit in developing a tool intended for use within individual authorities but which, when used by all, would provide directly comparable information. We recommend, therefore, that the agency should, as one of its first tasks, commission the design of such an analysis tool.

### 14.4.3 Library development plans


Having identified the present training need among the staff, the authority should then look forward to identify the needs that will arise in future. Here the key is the library's development plan. Public libraries in England are required to submit development plans to the Department for Culture, Media and Sport. In the other home countries there is no such requirement, although there can be few library authorities that do not have a well- worked-out plan for the development of their service. The training needs analysis, however, revealed that few development plans explicitly discuss training in information and communication technology. In the application to the Fund, the development plan should be used to determine the changing nature of the training needs, and in particular the need for training in advanced skills. The development plan should also set out a timetable for the roll-out of the Network.

### 14.4.4 Basic competence


The training plan submitted by each library authority should identify the extent of need for training to bring all staff up to the basic level of competence that we have described. It should identify the number of those who have already attained that level of competence and of those who have not. On the basis of this, it should set out a strategy to ensure that everyone reaches the required standard by 2002 or the completion of the local network, whichever is sooner.

<!-- PAGE: 92 -->

Clearly, it is essential to stage the acquisition of basic competences among the staff, to ensure that they have access to the equipment they will need to exercise their new skills. Without such access they will not be able to use and reinforce the practical skills they have acquired. The resulting training plan should be fully costed.

### 14.4.5 Advanced training


Once the basic training programme has been designed, the library authority will have a clearer idea how much money is available for advanced training. It should draw up its plans accordingly. Once again, these should be based on the analysis of need, allied to the library's development plan. The advanced training programme will have to be rather more detailed, as the agency will need to be able to assess the viability of the training proposed and the suitability of the providers selected. As we noted in section 11.5, it may be necessary to put some of the advanced training proposals out to referee.

### 14.4.6 Sustainability


Careful consideration needs to be given to libraries' plans to continue to develop and update the competences of their staff. The need for this has been amply demonstrated by the training provision that has taken place in the higher education sector. While it will not be possible for public libraries to make long-term commitments to future training, the agency could reasonably expect to see in each application some ideas for ensuring that the training is sustained.

### 14.4.7 Matched funding


The New Opportunities Fund is under an obligation to require applicants to cover at least some of the cost of training from their own resources. This matched funding could come in the form of direct contributions or contributions in kind, including:

- resources to enable staff to be released from front-line duties so that they can undergo

training;

- the cost of in-house training;

- the cost of purchasing training resources;

- the cost of the use of premises and equipment.

It is important to set the level of required matching so that it serves in some cases to lever additional resources from the local authority in recognition of the value that will be added to its services through the introduction of the New Library Network. At the same time, the level should not be so high that it deters those libraries with small or non-existent training budgets from applying to the scheme. We therefore recommend that the New Opportunities Fund should require applicants to contribute a minimum of 15 per cent of the total cost of the training programme from their own resources.

### 14.4.8 Staff replacement costs


The cost of replacing staff who are removed from front-line service so that they can undertake training is significant. An important thrust of this initiative is to ensure that the library staff that users come into contact with are competent in their use of information and communication technology. By definition, therefore, most of the staff who will receive training under this initiative will be providing front-line services. When they are taken out of that front line they will need to be replaced, and the library will have to bear the cost of employing relief staff or of increasing the hours of part-time staff to provide cover.

<!-- PAGE: 93 -->

This will be particularly significant in authorities with a large number of small service points, often staffed by only one or two people. In such authorities, staff replacement is a significant element in all training. In bigger service points it is often possible to cover for staff in the short term without making significant additional provision. It is regrettable, therefore, that the New Opportunities Fund appears highly unlikely to make funding available to provide staff cover for those attending courses.  Therefore, no recommendations relating to a part of the award made by the fund to be used towards staff release costs, in line with the Department for Culture, Media and Sport's discussion paper,2  are made here. It is likely that covering staff release costs will be the sole element in the authority's matching contribution. In some cases, the structure of the service is such that staff release costs are high, and we would hope that the NOF would consider making funding available in substantiated and exceptional cases - for example small service points with only one or two staff.

### 14.4.9 Refereeing


We envisage that many of the applications from library authorities will not be contentious and will be able to be assessed by the staff of the agency and passed to the Fund for approval by the board. There may, however, be some where it is appropriate to seek independent advice in order to conform to the Fund's directions from the Secretary of State. In such cases the agency should be allowed to decide whether it is more appropriate to establish panels to review applications or to use referees for individual applications.

## 14.5 Support actions


We believe that there are a small number of support actions that should be commissioned by the Fund, or undertaken on its behalf by the agency. Such actions will directly support the training initiative and will lead to better value for money. We have already mentioned the first support action: it is the development of a standard approach to the analysis of training needs. Such a nationally applicable tool will not only provide a consistent basis which will make the assessment of authorities' applications much easier, it will also avoid the cost of 240 library authorities developing their own approach. It may also be desirable to stimulate the development of national or regional initiatives that otherwise would not be commercially viable and which would otherwise leave a gap in the pattern of provision. The kinds of things we have in mind are the development of Welsh-language training materials, regional or national initiatives to develop training for specialist staff such as those with expertise in local studies or music, and the development of computer-based training packages. We believe that there could well be value in supporting cross-authority developments of this kind, and we therefore recommend that the Fund should set aside a modest amount in the region of £500,000 for such purposes.

# 15 Introduction to Part 3


The government has committed itself to 'ensuring that, where practicable, all public libraries should be connected to the National Grid for Learning - and trained to use it - by 2002'.4 At a minimum, this means providing Internet connections to about 4,000 public libraries in the UK. It is important to recognise that we are not proposing a specific networking model. For the purposes of producing cost estimates and so that the funding requirements can be assessed, we have presented options that meet the end-user requirements implied by the New Library Network vision, but it will be for the private sector to develop the actual networking approach to be used when it bids to supply New Library Network services (probably to groups of local authorities). # 16 Network options


Our remit is to define New Library Network services specified in terms of end-user requirements. Options have been generated and evaluated to identify:

- the type of service that should be procured to best meet the New Library Network

requirement;

- the approximate costs of implementation, to support analysis of the business case for the

Network;

- the consequent ongoing costs.

The main criteria used to assess these options are:

- the ability to meet grade-of-service requirements;

- the initial and ongoing costs of provision.

In this assessment, it must be understood that:

- a plausible outlin e Network design is used as a basis for analysis, but this should not be

taken as a proposal for the technical solution;

- identified costings are indicative only - in particular, they are based upon gross design

assumptions, and they make no allowance for savings that would arise from design optimisation or volume discounts.6 6 All cost estimates presented in this report apply only to the 4,000 fixed libraries in the UK. In addition, there are about 700 mobile libraries serving mainly rural communities. These libraries could also be connected to the New Library Network using, for example, satellite technology or mobile telephone technology. Alternatively, a fixed connection could be established in rural locations (say in the local school, post office or other public building) using either terrestrial or satellite technology. The approach taken would be for the relevant local bodies to decide.

## 16.1 Context


This assessment has been carried out in the context of two other important pieces of work:

- the Library and Information Commission report  New Library: The People's Network -

concerning the future role of libraries and their use of information and communication technology (ICT);

- the National Grid for Learning - concerning the role and implementation of ICT in

schools. The recommendations of New Library: The People's Network that are key to this assessment are that:

- public libraries should become key delivery points for local government, central

government and educational services and initiatives (including the NGfL);

- extensive use should be made of broadband, multimedia and interactive networked

applications in the delivery of these services;

- a dedicated or private broadband network should be implemented to connect all 4,000

libraries to each other and to national resources, thereby providing an infrastructure over which these services could be delivered. The features of the NGfL initiative that are key to this assessment are that:

- the provision of ICT to schools should be managed by a national agency, the British

Educational Communications and Technology agency (BECTa);

- networking between schools and national educational resources should be provided by

connection to the Internet, on the basis of specially agreed rates for ISDN 2 access. New Library: The People's Network and the NGfL have influenced our assessment in two ways: they have provided the basis of the application requirements and of the options for implementation of national network services that have been considered.

## 16.2 End-user services


New Library: The People's Network identifies a range of end-user services that will be provided through the New Library Network, including:

- lifelong learning and education;

- citizens' information and facilities for participating in society;

- facilities for business and the economy, training and emp loyment;

- facilities for researching community history and fostering community identity;

- the National Digital Library.

Development of applications to provide these services will be key to the development and growth of the New Library Network. Ultimately, end-users will define the Network by the applications it provides. A specification of the Network in end-user terms is markedly different from a specification in terms of the underlying technologies - as bearers or as a managed IP network, for example. Instead, the Network is specified as a managed network application service that integrates the functionality of underlying components to achieve functionality as perceived by end-users. A specification of this type is presented in Appendix 12. The key  features of this specification are:

- it defines the core and enhanced functionality that must be provided by all service

providers; <!-- PAGE: 102 --> - it requires that service providers must comply with minimum nationally agreed standards

for end-user functionality in the implementation of the core services (i.e. for the standard applications and the interactive/multimedia services described in Appendix 12.2 and 12.3), so that services from different providers are compatible and can interoperate to deliver national coverage;

- it provides for the definition and management of service configurations customised to

meet local requirements, i.e. as described in Appendix 12.4;

- it provides the option of using adaptive technologies to assist use by visually impaired

people;

- it identifies grade-of-service requirements for end-user functions that involve network-

based communications;

- it provides for supporting the implementation of local policies - for example, regarding

charging for services, protection of copyright, etc. From this point of view, the most obvious technical features of the New Library Network will be PCs or other terminals in libraries running a core suite of applications - for example, typical Internet, office automation and videoconferencing applications. These applications will provide for:

- services already available in some library authorities - for example, Internet access, word

processing and access to networked CD-roms;

- expected interactive and advanced multimedia services - as seen in local govern ment one-

stop shops, for example;

- development of future network services, if appropriate - for example, on the basis of so-

called 'universal client' standards as exemplified by the use of HTML and XML on the Internet. These terminals and applications w ill be supported by local and wide area networking services capable of providing end-users with acceptable grades of service for network- based communications and information retrieval.

## 16.3 Estimated cost of the New Library Network


A large-scale ICT project like the New Library Network will involve substantial initial and ongoing costs. However, there is at present no commodity market in managed services of the type specified for the Network in this document. Consequently, the approach that has been taken to estimating costs has been to construct an outline network design on the basis of off-the-shelf products and services offered by multiple suppliers. This approximate reference model, or public-sector comparator, will assist in assessing the value for money offered by bids from the managed service providers that are likely to appear. Initial and ongoing costs for the New Library Network have been estimated on the basis of:

- the ICT required in libraries to provide end-user services and the personnel input required

to support end-users in using these services, where the assumptions for the costs of these elements are based upon BECTa's recommendations to schools planning ICT implementation and are presented in Appendix 13;

- the requirements for network capacity that arise from the use of these services, where:

- assumptions regarding usage of the Network are presented in Appendix 14,

- assumptions regarding the physical characteristics that affect Network costs are presented

in Appendix 15;

- various approaches to procuring network capacity, where the options considered are

described and assessed in Appendix 16, on the basis of commodity pricing information from the current BT network services catalogue.

16.4 Procurement issues
Investigation of procurement issues for the New Library Network has focused upon
achieving maximum economies of scale and minimum procurement costs, while still
allowing flexibility in specification and implementation at the local authority level.
Figure 16.1 indicates that there are three basic categories of cost where economies of
scale should be sought:

<!-- PAGE: 106 -->

- backbone networking - this technically consists of inner and outer backbone services;

- regional networking - i.e. authority-wide and/or metropolitan area networking;

- in-library ICT - i.e. computing equipment and software in libraries.

### 16.4.1 Backbone networking


It is a feature of the technologies used as the basis for estimating costs in this report that a backbone would in practice be likely to consist of:

- an inner (very-high-bandwidth) element, the responsibilities of which would probably

include routing of traffic at national level;

- an outer (lower-bandwidth) element, the responsibilities of which would probably include

multiplexing of traffic from multiple library authorities so that it can be presented to the router network. Alternative technologies may allocate responsibilities differently. However, this distinction between the inner and outer backbone is likely to remain significant, as the costs of implementation of the outer backbone are likely to vary according to geographic region. Effective competition exists for the supplying of backbone services with the coverage envisaged. For inner backbone services, however, it is likely to be most cost-effective to procure the service from a single supplier, as:

- seeking to distribute this function between multiple suppliers would be likely to increase

initial and ongoing costs by requiring duplication of infrastructure and management services, as well as introducing new interconnection services;

- at this level of capacity, there is no commodity market in any other form of managed

service (for example, a 'shared' network service such as a managed IP network) upon which to base any expectation that the cost of duplication could be avoided. Economies of scale also, in general, point to choosing a single service provider for the outer backbone service. For example, on the basis of BT pricing, providing a choice of supplier at each of the twenty-three backbone access points would increase networking costs by approximately £730,000 initially and by £1.8 million per year thereafter to achieve the same level of capacity. However, geographic variation in the costs of supplying outer backbone services may impact on this in two ways:

- the cross-subsidies that would be implicit in a uniform backbone connection charge may

not be sustainable - for example, volume discounts and economies of scale may not be sufficient to make the service cost-effective in areas (such as major cities) where networking costs are low;

- some regional suppliers may be able to offer very low costs in their area of operation and

thus may again make a national charge uncompetitive. In some urban areas, such as London, this issue may be largely sidestepped by developing a metropolitan area network (MAN) (or using an existing MAN) that reduces or eliminates the need for backbone connection. However, in general, it is likely to be necessary to allow:

- either regional pricing by a national provider so that the national service always

represents good value

- or competition for supply of outer backbone services at the regional level.

It therefore seems likely that the procurement strategy will need to involve some element of market testing to ensure best value for money for and participation by the whole library service. It should also be noted that further substantial economies of scale may be achieved at both the technical and the management levels by integrating the New Library Network with one or more other national networks - for example, JANET/SuperJANET <!-- PAGE: 107 --> technology and UK Educational and Research Network Agency (UKERNA) management. At present there is no commercially available managed network service tha t meets the requirements of the New Library Network for national connectivity, in terms of providing a combination of:

- usage-independent charging;

- facilities to guarantee capacity to the Network and synchronous delivery of interactive

applications such as live two-way video;

- the facility for each authority to subscribe separately on the basis of published tariffs.

If, as may happen in the near future, such services do become available, they would have substantial attractions as platforms for New Library Network national connectivity. In particular, they would largely eliminate the requirement for:

- formally establishing a national backbone for the New Library Network;

- government entering into the funding commitments that a formal backbone would imply;

- establishing or using the services of a network management agency.

Such services would also greatly facilitate the bundling of network services with New Library Network end-user services.

### 16.4.2 Authority-wide networking


At this level, while effective competition exists in many areas, it does not exist nationwide. Also, cross-subsidy issues arising from any attempt to achieve uniform connection costs (i.e. independent of distance) are likely to be pronounced. Regional price variations therefore appear inevitable. Also, an authority network comprising twenty Megastream bearers (as will be the case for the New Library Network) will represent both a substantial network in its own right and a substantial upgrade to most local authority networks. As such, it is likely that local authorities (or, even better, groups of authorities) will in many instances be able to get substantial volume discounts in their own right. In these instances, any benefits of national procurement are likely to be small. Also, a national procurement would have great difficulty in integrating or otherwise dealing with existing local authority networking. Consequently, national implementation at this level is not generally recommended. It is expected that local authorities will:

- either design and procure bearer networks

- or get managed service providers to do so on their behalf, using the most economically

advantageous infrastructure and tariffs available - by bundling procurement of these services and of end-user services, for example.

### 16.4.3 In-library ICT


In-library ICT will have three main functions:

- it will provide the end-user devices through which New Library Network applications

will be accessed;

- it will perform the necessary network management and interfac ing functions to connect

these devices to other devices on the Network, both inside the building and beyond;

- it will provide storage for local collections and other information.

On the basis of the assumptions in this document, the New Library Network will lead to a highly dispersed population of 40,000 terminals. On average, each library authority will <!-- PAGE: 108 --> have approximately 200 terminals located at 20 sites. This leads to a number of economic issues concerning:

- purchase and maintenance;

- management and support;

- technical compatibility and interoperability.

Local procurement at the library authority level has little chance of maximising economies of scale in these key areas. This is particularly the case for management and support, where geographic dispersion greatly increases costs. Periodic competition for 'regional' monopolies is one means by which value for money might be improved. This may be complex to arrange organisationally, because of the need for co-operation among numerous library authorities. However, the existing regional groupings in England might provide a mechanism for such co-operation, as might analogous groupings in Scotland, Northern Ireland and Wales. A similar effect might be achieved without explicit 'regional' co-operation b y using the BECTa model of 'kitemarking' ICT service providers. In areas where economies of scale in management and support are difficult to achieve, there would be a tendency for the kitemarked service provider that wins the first 'regional' competition to win in other 'regions' too, through achieving economies of scale in ICT management and support. Adopting the BECTa model also provides the possibility of exploiting an existing national ICT organisation to manage the following issues:

- National standardisation at the technical level - albeit to a markedly different (and more

demanding) standard than the current NGfL platforms. The requirements for the New Library Network would have to be defined by a national body on behalf of the library service. However, as Appendix 12 provides for customisation of a basic specification, this activity should focus upon establishing standards for the applications described in Appendix 12.2 and 12.3 on a basis that gives individual authorities substantial technical flexibility to accommodate local needs and issues of existing technology.

- Transaction costs of procurement - where the national agency might manage national

competitions to kitemark New Library Network suppliers.

- Achieving interoperability with the NGfL a nd other educational initiatives.

## 16.5 Service specifications and contracting


New Library Network services can be specified in end-user terms that are independent of the underlying technology. However, implementing these services in the national and distributed way that is planned will have implications for procurement and/or pricing. In principle, there are three separate elements for which local authorities are contracting:

- In-library ICT services. These would comprise a managed network application platform,

as specified in Appendix 12, which would be designed to meet the needs of the library service nationally while allowing local variation, providing the following services:

- the ability to host a standard set of applications, and facilities to allow these applications

to be installed and managed from the national network;

- the ability to host additional applications as specified by an authority;

- the ability to host the local data, information and other content produced, procured and

distributed by the authority;

- facilities to manage user accounts, network configurations, applications, and data installed

on the application platform;

- facilities to manage user access to information according to the authority's local policy;

- directory and multipoint application facilities (especially for multipoint

videoconferencing, for example) to allow users within the authority to communicate and to participate in collaborative activities.

- Authority-wide networking services. These would comprise commodity network services,

contracted for on whichever basis is deemed to be most appropriate, providing:

- appropriate guarantees of bandwidth between central library and branch library sites;

- appropriate access bandwidth to the Internet.

- Backbone network services. These would comprise a broadband switched or routed

network optimised to meet inter-authority communication requirements and providing the following services:

- guaranteed grades of service (in bandwidth terms) for specified virtual circuits in the

backbone network;

- root directory and multipoint application facilities (especially for multipoint

videoconferencing, for example) to allow users in different authorities to communicate and to participate in collaborative activities. In practice, a local authority could bundle the procurement of these components into a single managed service agreement according to the specification presented in Appendix 12. Equally, a local authority could elect to separate these elements. In each case, the end- user service specification would still be applicable to the procurement of in-library ICT from a kitemarked supplier. The principal difference would be that:

- in the integrated managed service approach, the authority would contract for end-user

grade-of-service guarantees;

- in the separated approaches, the local authority would contract for network capacity and

would then assume responsibility for this level of capacity meeting end-user grade-of- service targets. As discussed in section 16.4.1, bundling of managed backbone services and network end- user services is potentially of substantial benefit to the New Library Network. However, if there is a substantial delay in the emergence of managed services, it will be necessary to consider whether to wait for the market to provide them or to proceed with a centrally procured service.

## 16.6 Conclusions

<!-- PAGE: 110 -->

This chapter has considered four aspects of the New Library Network vision:

- translation of the vision into a Network service specification expressed in end- user terms;

- estimation of implementation and ongoing costs for delivery of the service;

- requirements for national co-ordination in planning and managing the service;

- issues affecting procurement of the service.

The conclusions reached are as follows:

### 16.6.1 Network service specification


The vision for the New Library Network is that it should to be able to serve a wide variety of social and commercial requirements. At this stage, the computer applications that would meet many of these requirements do not yet exist. However, the examples of leading libraries - for example, in Croydon, Cambridge, Solihull etc. - give an insight into the form that these services may ultimately take. For the time being, however, a managed network application platform is the most appropriate model for an end-user specification of the New Library Network, and such a specification is presented in Appendix 12. A key feature of this type of specification is that, from an end-user viewpoint, local and network services are indistinguishable. The key consequences of this feature are:

- the scale of procurement is defined by the number and locations of users specified;

- extending the scope of network applications to regional and/or national coverage is

achieved by specification of a network grade of service, either in application terms (if these are known) or in bandwidth terms (as has been done in Appendix 12);

- in principle, user locations are not constrained, and hence New Library services can be

required to be delivered to any location;

- it is a service provider's responsibility to find network design and other technical

solutions to meet the service specifications. A further consequence is that the New Library Network specification leads to an impression of a closed network user community. In one sense this is true, and is what is required as part of the Network vision - i.e. to provide dedicated resources. However:

- provision is made in the specification for controlled interconnection to other network

communities (primarily the Internet and the NGfL, but others can be specified as the need arises) through gateway services;

- the New Library Network is specified as a virtual private network, and therefore does not

require dedicated physical network resources for its implementation. These features allow the Network user community to have controlled access to the resources of other communities, and vice versa. They also allow the New Library Network to share physical network resources with other networks - those of local authorities, for example.

### 16.6.2 Cost of delivery


Various network design options have been evaluated to estimate costs. It has been concluded that, together with local management of ICT, a broadband virtual private network solution offers the most cost-effective reference model. Consequently, this approach has been used for the estimates. Absolute estimates of cost were presented in Table 16.1. In addition to limitations in accuracy, due to their being based upon list prices, there are two further points to note about these:

<!-- PAGE: 111 -->

- while the visionary nature of the New Library Network leaves uncertainty about the exact

form of the applications and patterns of usage that will be encountered, the cost estimates are relatively robust to deviations from the gross assumptions that have been made;

- when compared with the costs of the baseline Network design, they can be viewed as

offering good value for money. The first of these points is so because:

- ICT costs are largely independent of geographical distribution and are predictable because

they vary more or less directly with the number of terminals;

- network costs are again both robust to variation and predictable because they are

principally a function of the number of network connections and of bandwidth requirements. Bandwidth requirements for each connection are a function of the types of application hosted by the Network and are largely independent of the number of users of these applications. Consequently, networking costs are virtually constant in all scenarios, while ICT cost elements vary proportionally with the total Network user population. The private virtual network solution can be considered to be good value for money in comparison with narrowband alternatives, because:

- even at list prices it has nearly identical initial costs and only approximately double

ongoing costs for far greater functionality;

- it enables the complete New Library Network vision to be implemented, and provides an

enabling platform for innovative and novel network applications in ways that narrowband alternatives never could. The relative size of the overall cost premium for the private virtual network solution is reduced further when ICT costs are taken into account, as both narrowband and broadband implementations have similar costs in this respect. Consideration has also been given to the potential effect of discounts (similar to those obtainable for the NGfL) upon this cost differential. In this area, however, it has been concluded that discounts would have to be agreed for broadband, as opposed to narrowband, services if these were to be of substantial benefit.

### 16.6.3 National co-ordination


Investigation of economy-of-scale issues has identified the need for co-ordination at national level in procurement of backbone network service and in-library ICT. With respect to procurement of in-library ICT, technical factors relating to interoperability between authorities also provide strong motivation for national co-ordination. The responsibilities involved in these two aspects of national co-ordination are similar to the existing responsibilities of UKERNA and BECTa in the educational sector. However, it must be emphasised that, whatever agency is made responsible for these aspects, strong direction on technical standards and rules of participation will be essential to the success of the New Library Network. In particular:

- a network management organisation would have to ensure the security and integrity of the

national network, adequate implementation of core network resources, and observance of rules of connection to the network;

- a BECTa-like organisation would have to administer a kitemarking and compliance-

approval regime that guaranteed technical interoperability between different network service providers. Internet standards, standards defined for library systems and content standards will assist national co-ordination and should be adopted. However, even using these standards, the <!-- PAGE: 112 --> kitemarking and compliance role will be particularly demanding. In practice, it may be achievable only by defining a reference technical implementation and requiring suppliers seeking to vary from this implementation to demonstrate comprehensively that they will achieve interoperability with the reference platform.

### 16.6.4 Service procurement


The service specification presented in Appendix 12 offers purchasing authorities many options for coming together in purchasing consortia, customising the service to local requirements and/or bundling various aspects of network connectivity (i.e. local and national) with delivery of end-user services. It is for local authorities to decide which approach best meets their needs, subject to the constraints imposed by the need for national interoperability. One factor is clear, however: it is highly unlikely that networking charges will be uniform across the UK. Consequently, regional variations in prices will have to be addressed in managing and funding the New Library Network.

# 17 Funding options and financial assessment


Over the medium term (at least) the New Library Network will operate alongside the existing largely paper-based library service. It will therefore be necessary to find funding largely additional to that already provided to libraries, in order to meet the costs of the Network. These costs will comprise both initial capital costs and operating costs. This chapter assesses:

- the potential sources of funding for the New Library Network;

- the financial viability of the Network, based on a number of possible revenue and co st

scenarios. Funding for the New Library Network could potentially come from a range of sources, including:

- the public sector;

- third-party (commercial) revenues;

- a philanthropist (private or corporate).

In addition, reduced telecommunications charges could considerably reduce the ongoing cost of the Network. Figure 17.1 shows the range of funding possibilities and the body which might generate the funding.

The purchasers of authority-wide networking and in-library ICT are likely to be either

Responsibility for generating third-party revenues is perhaps more appropriately placed

• it transfers the risk associated with demand uncertainties from the public to the private

• the private-sector provider will have the strongest incentive and the greatest ability to

• encourage use of the Network;
• promote growth in applications and information sources;
• allow the Network to become self-sustainable over the longer term;
• do not compromise the role of the library as a trusted, impartial source of information;
• support government objectives in respect of enabling social inclusion in access to ICT and
information sources.
For reasons that will become more apparent below, these key criteria suggest that public
funds will be an important source of financial support, though third-party revenues also
have a significant and potentially growing role to play.

Below we provide a summary appraisal of the potential for public-sector and third-party revenues. While there may be some scope to attract philanthropic donations, given the public-service element of the Network, it is not possible to assess their significance. Similarly, while specially discoun ted telecommunications charges would be a major benefit, the likelihood of these becoming available and the scale of reductions that might be achieved cannot be reliably assessed, as these depend on government persuasion. ## 17.1 Public-sector funding options


Public-sector funding options score very highly against most of the funding criteria outlined above. In particular, they reduce the need to charge for services not created with New Opportunities Fund support, and so encourage the use of the Network and promote government objectives in terms of preventing social exclusion. However, the extent of public monies that will be available remains unclear. At present the following public-sector sources seem likely to be available to help fund the New Library Network:

- A challenge fund similar to the DCMS/Wolfson Fund which has pledged £3 million per

year from 1998 to 2000 for ICT infrastructure in libraries. Availability of DCMS/Wolfson funding is at present limited to England. Similar funds could in principle be set up in the other home countries.

- The Heritage Lottery Fund and the New Opportunities Fund, although at present public

library ICT infrastructure projects are not eligible for this funding.

- Library authority budgets. At £18 million, current ICT expenditures account for only 2-3

per cent of total library expenditure. Recent real reductions in total library budgets and the need to maintain paper-based systems mean that these budgets are unlikely to be a significant source of funding.

- Local authority, central government and other agency funding for educational and

economic development activities which the New Library Network could help support.

- EU regional development programmes, particularly in rural and economically poor

regions.

- Private Finance Initiative (PFI) funding. In England and Wales, central government has

made available additional revenue support (also known as 'PFI credits') to fund the capital component of PFI schemes. In order to secure this revenue support, local authorities have to submit an Outline Business Case to the relevant government department. In England this is the Department of the Environment, Transport and the Regions (DETR), which also includes all DCMS schemes - i.e. the DCMS does not have its own specific PFI credit allocation. The total sum of PFI credits available in the current year (1998/99) is £500 million, of which the DETR's share of is £200 million. For 1999/2000 the total allocation has been increased to £800 million, and the DETR's share will be £250 million. The Welsh Office deals with all Welsh PFI projects. The DETR's PFI credit allocation is currently under strong pressure from a range of PFI schemes that are coming forward - principally waste management and transport schemes, which by their very nature tend to be relatively expensive. Realistically, the scope for the DCMS securing a large allocation from the DETR to fund the necessary infrastructure investment for the New Library Network in the short term will be limited. Therefore <!-- PAGE: 116 --> central government (possibly the DCMS itself) may be the only viable alternative to secure the necessary PFI funding. Reflecting the government's prioritising of educational projects, the Department for Education and Employment's allocation of PFI credits will increase from £130 million to £350 million in 1999, so there may be an opportunity to target some of that money if the eventual scheme has a defined schools link. Assuming that such government support is forthcoming, there will be opportunities for groups of local authorities to collaborate, perhaps on a regional basis, to apply for funding; but clearly the ground rules will need to be agreed in advance, to ensure that approved projects meet certain common objectives. Alternatively, standard minimum- service-level contracts for New Library Network procurements could be developed, and an added advantage of this is that it should reduce the PFI transaction costs.

## 17.2 Third-party revenues


Potential third-party revenue opportunities for the New Library Network include transaction-based services, for which a charge might be levied on either the user or the provider of the service, and advertising. These opportunities clearly meet the first three of the funding criteria set out above. By increasing the range of services supported by the New Library Network, they encourage the use of the Network. Since they represent a new and ongoing revenue source, they also contribute to the long-term sustainability of the Network. In assessing the scope for third-party revenue generation, it is important to note that the New Library Network will represent just one of an increasing number of networks providing access to online information and services. Among the alternatives are the Internet, information kiosks and digital TV. Technological developments are also increasing the number of outlets at which online services can be accessed - access points will include the home, the office, educational institutions, supermarkets, post offices etc. In developing commercial revenues, it will therefore be important to target services which build upon the advantages the New Library Network could offer over alternative delivery media, namely:

- the numbers and mix of people visiting public libraries (see box below);

- staff skilled in providing information services;

- a building/space regarded as a safe and trusted environment to which access is not

restricted and which increasingly offers user-friendly opening hours.

## 17.4 Conclusions


This chapter has demonstrated the scale of the funding requirement for the New Library Network, which has an net present value of £529 million over the eight-year period from mid-1999 to mid-2007. Sensitivity tests on the target scenario show that:

- a slower roll-out period of five years reduces the scale of the funding requirement to

around £477 million;

- negotiating NGfL-like discounts could reduce the funding requirement to around £73

million;

- reducing the total number of terminals installed from 40,000 to 30,000 leaves the funding

requirement broadly unchanged. The requirement for public funding for the New Library Network is to be expected, given the Network's essentially public-service roles of providing:

- low-cost or free access to electronic information services and use of computers to those

who would not otherwise have access to such services - the information 'have-nots';

- wider public access to lifelong learning and education services;

- a distribution channel for community information and services provided by local

government, and for promoting local/home-country democracy;

- a distribution channel for central government services.

In addition, the dual provision of paper-based and electronic information services in libraries for the next five to ten years means it is unrealistic to expect substantial funding for the New Library Network to come from existing library budgets. The funding requirement should be covered by central, home country and local government monies. The local government contribution could come from a number of sources, including the savings from use of existing local government networks and from substitution of electronic for other (for example face-to-face) communication with residents. The New Library Network may also benefit from funding under initiatives to promote links between citizens and the new governments in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. However, a substantial proportion of the money will need to come from central government. At this stage the most likely source of such funds is PFI credits. To demonstrate value for money in applications for this funding, it will be important that local authorities collaborate to mount joint regional bids.

## 18.1 Network roll-out

The network approach suggested in this report is a reference project which is likely to
fulfil user requirements (as currently understood) at least cost, and which would be
scaleable with respect to increases in numbers of terminals, limited increases in
multimedia usage, and changes in the geographic distribution of terminals. When the
provision of networking services is put out to tender, the private sector can be expected to
offer different implementation solutions, linked more closely to the existing situation in
different local authority areas and based on a more detailed understanding of end-user and
local authority requirements.
As discussed in the previous chapters, we envisage the procurement process working as
follows:
• backbone services will be procured by a national agency, although this may not be
necessary if commercial managed services become available;
• authority-wide networking and in-library ICT will be procured by local authorities (or
groups of them);
• local authorities will hold competitive tenders, using 'kitemarked' suppliers and/or subject
to the service meeting national standards in terms of end-user requirements;
• services will be implemented in a phased manner.
There are two dimensions to phasing of implementation:
• implementation by region;
• implementation of authority networks and the backbone.
The most conventional implem entation approach would be to roll out authority-wide
connectivity region by region. This has a number of advantages:

<!-- PAGE: 128 -->

- roll-out of the Network and training for librarians can be co-ordinated in a cost-effective

manner - i.e. so that training coincides with or is undertaken only a short time in advance of Network roll-out;

- implementation can be timed to suit local circumstances in terms of funding availability

and other related local initiatives, say in the educational sector;

- lessons learned from earl y implementations can be used in planning subsequent Network

roll-out;

- it is similar to the approach being contemplated for the NGfL.

We suggest that initially a number of pilots are implemented covering a diversity of regions in terms of population density, existing provision of networks and ICT in libraries, and geographic location. For example, an initial implementation in rural areas could build on the networks and ICT being deployed in libraries in Wales under the Llwybr-Pathway project and in educational establishments in the west of Scotland under the University for the Highlands initiative. Likewise there are developments in populous urban areas in the North-East, North-West, South-East and Midlands of England which the New Library Network could enhance by providing additional terminals and higher-bandwidth connections between libraries. It will also be important to undertake some initial roll-out in areas where there is currently little provision of ICT and network services in libraries, in order to gain experience of the particular problems that may arise and to provide a test bed for the co-ordination of Network roll-out and librarian training. Turning next to national connectivity, in terms of the services that are currently available, there is a choice of either using the Internet or implementing a dedicated national backbone. The Internet is a low-risk approach, given the currently uncertain demand for national connectivity, but, against this, it cannot offer guaranteed service levels for broadband applications. On the basis of current service offerings we favour the implementation of a national backbone. This national backbone would involve:

- centralised funding, which would probably need to come from central government, given

the practical difficulties in obtaining agreement to a common fund raised from all local authorities;

- a national agency to procure and manage the backbone service.

These arrangements will take some time to put in place. It is possible that commercial managed network services that could meet the New Library Network's requirements for national connectivity will appear during this time. Such services could offer considerable advantages. A national network management agency would not be required, and services could be purchased by local authorities on the basis of an agreed tariff. However, it is uncertain when and at what price such managed network services might enter the market. We therefore suggest that during 1999:

- the possibility of central government f unding for national connectivity is pursued; but

- decisions about the means by which this is implemented are delayed until the end of

1999; and,

- in the interim, the Internet is used to provide national connectivity.

# 19 A national agency


Each of the three main parts of this report has identified the need for a national agency to carry forward the work of implementing the New Library Network. The aim of this chapter is to bring these recommendations together, to identify the functions that such an agency should fulfil.

## 19.1 Functions of a national agency


There are considerable similarities in the functions proposed in order to develop the content creation and training strands of the New Opportunities Fund, and the relevant task groups have each recommended an agency to administer the awards on behalf of the Fund. In this they have been guided by the desire of the Fund's board to maintain only a small headquarters staff, contracting out functions where appropriate. The main function of the agency would be to administer the awards process: advising intending applicants, dealing with applications as they come in and, with the help of external referees, making recommendations to the Fund on those projects that are worth funding. The agency could also manage the payments to successful applicants, although it seems likely that it will be more cost-effective for the Fund to handle these payments through its headquarters operation. There may, however, be a role for the agency in monitoring the progress made by successful applicants, advising the Fund accordingly so that payments are made only in respect of work successfully accomplished. The agency would also be required to monitor the overall progress of the initiative, reporting back regularly to the Fund. Both task groups also identified a number of support actions that will be required to ensure the overall effectiveness of the initiatives. These support actions could be undertaken or managed by the agency. The implementation activities needed to take the New Library Network infrastructure forward are rather different. Here there are significant procurement and (longer-term) management functions, as well as a need to maintain quite strict technical standards.

## 19.2 Characteristics of a national agency


Public library services are closely identified with their local communities, and local authorities will play the most important role in making the New Library Network a reality. Any national agency should therefore be constituted in such a way as to encourage the active and enthusiastic involvement of local authorities. The involvement of local government throughout the UK is essential to an effective implementation of the New Library Network vision. The agency should cover the whole of the UK. The New Library Network will be UK-wide, and the implementation activities should be carried out in a consistent UK-wide manner. Full account should, however, be taken of the different legislative and administrative arrangements in each of the four home countries, and some mechanism should exist to ensure that these national requirements and differences can be accommodated. In due course it may also be desirable to take similar account of the particular needs of English regions.
