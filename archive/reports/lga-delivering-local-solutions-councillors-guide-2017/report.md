## Foreword

Millions of people use libraries: in England alone public libraries were visited 225 million times in 2014/15 – more than visits to Premier League football games, the cinema, and the top 10 UK tourist attractions combined. Libraries are trusted spaces, free to enter and open to all. In them, people explore and share reading, information, knowledge and culture. We know that people value the range of books, digital and other resources available, as well as the trained staff who help them. But how people use libraries is changing. If we want our services to continue to be at the heart of communities for generations to come libraries must change as well.

> ‘Libraries change lives for the better. They not only provide access to books and other literature but also help people to help themselves and improve their opportunities, bring people together, and provide practical support and guidance. As a locally accountable service, they are well-placed to respond to local needs and issues.’  
> — *Libraries Deliver: Ambition for Public Libraries in England, 2016-2021* ([https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/libraries-deliver-ambition-for-public-libraries-in-england-2016-to-2021](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/libraries-deliver-ambition-for-public-libraries-in-england-2016-to-2021))

Written for all councillors who have an interest in supporting the development of public library services, this guide will take you through the how and why of transformation in your area. Whether you are a portfolio holder with responsibility for libraries and wider cultural services, or a ward councillor who has campaigned tirelessly to keep a local library open in a period of cost-cutting and rationalisation, it will set out ways in which you can ensure your library service excels and meets the needs of your communities.

In continuing to deliver the very best services libraries face unprecedented pressures in response to changing and increasing customer demand. Despite budget reductions and tight fiscal constraint, they have to demonstrate that they remain economic, efficient and effective and contribute to achieving important strategic corporate objectives. Old ways of working are being challenged and new models of service delivery are being developed. There has never been so much change and innovation within the library service.

Alongside this, many library services are seeing the enormous benefits that technological and digital innovation can bring – giving them opportunities to offer new services, bring in new customers and extend their reach into communities.

<!-- PAGE: 5 -->

Beyond this, libraries are being seen increasingly as vital community hubs – bringing people together and giving them access to the services and support they need to live better lives. As part of a ‘Libraries First’ approach, many councils recognise that library services are critical to the effective delivery of a wide range of desired strategic outcomes – what the joint LGA/DCMS Leadership for Libraries Taskforce nationally has identified as:

- cultural and creative enrichment
- increased reading and literacy
- improved digital access and literacy
- helping everyone achieve their full potential
- healthier and happier lives
- greater prosperity
- stronger, more resilient communities.

There is a statutory requirement for your council to deliver a ‘comprehensive and efficient library service’. This should not be seen as a burden, but a massive opportunity – one in which you can play an active and influential role.

## Executive summary

Public libraries are funded to provide a unique ‘cradle-to-grave’ service. Through their buildings, outreach activities and online digital services, they are being seen increasingly as community hubs – bringing people together and giving them access to a greater breadth and depth of services and support than ever before.

Many councils are responding to tight fiscal constraints and reductions in local government funding by challenging traditional service models and re-designing how their library services are delivered. Change and innovation are rife. There are many different delivery models now operating in England, with local communities playing an increasingly important role in running, supporting and shaping services. In addition, new technology and digital resources are helping libraries to attract new customers, build new connections and extend their reach.

Whatever delivery model is chosen, it is vital to build on what is already working well – including a vibrant physical presence and strong digital service. It is also important that services:

- are based on a robust and realistic assessment of community needs
- have a clear purpose and strategic direction that aligns with wider corporate objectives
- have effective political leadership, community engagement and staff involvement
- are delivered by a skilled and confident workforce
- are supported by sustainable, long-term funding
- are subject to ongoing scrutiny and review.

The joint LGA/DCMS Leadership for Libraries Taskforce published *Libraries Deliver: Ambition for Public Libraries in England 2016-2021* which set out a shared vision for public library services in England. This councillor handbook is designed to support you as an elected member in championing, planning, funding, delivering and scrutinising your council’s library strategy.

## Introduction

> ‘Libraries are places where democracy, freedom and equality are not only respected but celebrated. The unique added value of a library is that it makes these outcomes universally accessible and relevant on equal terms to all members of the community in a way that is highly cost-effective for the public purse.’  
> — *Libraries Deliver: Ambition for Public Libraries in England 2016-2021*

In 2014, William Sieghart published his *Independent Library Report for England*, which called for national leadership and a refreshed vision for public library services. In response, the Department for Culture, Media and Sport (DCMS) and the Local Government Association (LGA) jointly established the Libraries Taskforce in March 2015 to provide that leadership and help library services across England to innovate, partner and transform.

In December 2016, the Taskforce published *Libraries Deliver: Ambition for Public Libraries in England 2016 to 2021*. Endorsed by central and local government, this document provides a focus for the future development of public library services across England. It highlights the seven key outcomes that libraries deliver:

1. **Cultural and creative enrichment**
2. **Increased reading and literacy**
3. **Improved digital access and literacy**
4. **Helping everyone achieve their full potential**
5. **Healthier and happier lives**
6. **Greater prosperity**
7. **Stronger, more resilient communities.**

### Enterprising Libraries: Supporting Local Businesses

The British Library and 10 library services across the country form the Business & Intellectual Property (IP) Centre Network: Birmingham, Exeter (Devon), Hull, Leeds, Liverpool, Manchester, Newcastle, Norwich (Norfolk), Sheffield, and Northamptonshire.

Funded by the Department for Communities and Local Government (DCLG), Arts Council England and the British Library, the Enterprising Libraries programme demonstrated how libraries can act as engines of local economic growth. An independent evaluation of the network by Economic Insight found that between 2013 and 2015, the BIPC network helped create:

- nearly 1,700 new businesses
- 4,200 new jobs
- a net GVA contribution of £38 million to local economies.

Crucially, the centres attracted diverse entrepreneurs: 64% of users were women, 32% were from Black, Asian and Minority Ethnic (BAME) backgrounds, and 10% were previously unemployed.

### Case Study: Clapham Library, London Borough of Lambeth

![Clapham Library](assets/clapham_library.jpg)

Clapham Library opened in 2012 as part of an innovative public-private partnership between Lambeth Council and Cathedral Group (now U+I). The mixed-use development delivered a high-specification 20,000 sq ft public library alongside 136 new homes and commercial space at zero capital cost to the council.

The building, designed by Studio Egret West, follows a continuous spiral ramp around a central atrium. In addition to extensive book collections and public computing facilities, Clapham Library incorporates:

- a 120-seat performance space
- meeting rooms and community spaces for hire
- a cafe
- an integrated NHS health clinic.

Issues and visits rose dramatically following opening, demonstrating how co-location and striking design can revitalise public library usage.

### Healthier and Happier Lives

Public libraries play a major preventative role in public health and wellbeing. Libraries are safe, non-clinical community spaces where people can find trusted health information, reduce social isolation, and engage in therapeutic activities.

Many libraries participate in schemes like **Reading Well: Books on Prescription**, developed by The Reading Agency and the Society of Chief Librarians (SCL) in partnership with health charities and clinical bodies. Reading Well provides quality-assured self-help reading for people experiencing common mental health conditions, dementia, and chronic health challenges, as well as specific lists for young people.

In addition, libraries host:

- memory cafes and reminiscence groups for people living with dementia and their carers
- health literacy and digital health access sessions
- knit-and-natter and social clubs tackling loneliness
- blood pressure self-monitoring kiosks and public health drop-in clinics.

<!-- PAGE: 11 -->

### Case Study: Somerset ‘Glass Box’

Somerset Library Service transformed an underused space within Taunton Library into the **Glass Box** – a public digital innovation and enterprise space. Developed with funding from Arts Council England, the space offers:

- access to 3D printers, scanners, and coding technology
- digital skills sessions for all ages and backgrounds
- business advice and intellectual property support for local start-ups.

Delivered by library staff with volunteer mentors and university partners, the Glass Box showcases how public library services can equip communities with digital manufacturing and technological capabilities.

## Making the case for libraries

As a local councillor you are well placed to champion the transformative role of public libraries within your authority. To make a persuasive case to council colleagues, health commissioners, and local partners, you need to articulate how libraries support wider strategic priorities:

- **Economic recovery and town centre vitality:** Libraries drive footfall to high streets and shopping centres, support jobseekers, and nurture local enterprise.
- **Educational attainment and social mobility:** Libraries support early language development through Rhymetime, maintain children's reading over the summer through the Summer Reading Challenge, and offer quiet study space and free resources for disadvantaged students.
- **Adult social care and health prevention:** By reducing social isolation, providing health information, and supporting carers, libraries help people remain independent and ease pressures on acute health and care services.
- **Digital inclusion and customer access:** Libraries provide the public access computers, free Wi-Fi, and assisted digital support that enable residents to access digital government and welfare services.

<!-- PAGE: 13 -->

### Encouraging a ‘Libraries First’ Culture

A ‘Libraries First’ approach encourages all council directorates and external public sector partners to consider libraries as their default front door and delivery partner:

- Before investing in new public access buildings or consulting on new service points, ask: *can this be co-located or delivered through our public library network?*
- When commissioning preventative public health or adult learning programmes, ask: *can libraries host or deliver this intervention?*
- When designing digital public services, ask: *how will our libraries support residents who lack digital access or confidence?*

<!-- PAGE: 14 -->

## Encouraging common design principles

In *Libraries Deliver: Ambition for Public Libraries in England 2016-2021*, the Libraries Taskforce set out a set of common design principles to guide the planning, delivery, and transformation of local library services.

Libraries should be:

- **Free to enter and open to all:** Providing an open, welcoming, and non-judgmental civic space.
- **Community-focused:** Rooted in local needs and designed through genuine community consultation.
- **Professionally supported:** Managed and delivered by skilled, knowledgeable staff with access to continuing professional development.
- **Digitally enabled:** Offering high-speed broadband, modern public computing, and 24/7 digital services.
- **Collaborative:** Actively seeking out partnerships with public, private, and voluntary sector bodies.
- **Sustainable:** Supported by diverse, resilient income streams and cost-effective delivery models.

<!-- PAGE: 15 -->

### Useful ‘Design Criteria’

When reviewing your service, consider whether your libraries meet modern expectations:

- **Location and accessibility:** Are libraries situated in central, accessible locations with strong transport links and high footfall?
- **Interior design and layout:** Are spaces flexible, welcoming, and well-lit, with distinct zones for quiet reading, study, children, and community activities?
- **Opening hours:** Do opening hours reflect modern working patterns, including evenings and weekends?
- **Digital infrastructure:** Is Wi-Fi reliable and fast, with adequate power points and mobile charging facilities?
- **Stock quality:** Is book stock fresh, attractive, and responsive to community demand, supported by effective stock rotation?

<!-- PAGE: 16 -->

### Co-Location and Shared Facilities

Co-locating libraries with other public and community services offers significant advantages:

- reduced building and operating overheads through shared facilities
- increased customer footfall and convenience for residents
- opportunities for integrated service delivery (e.g. libraries with customer service centres, leisure centres, health clinics, or arts venues).

### Case Study: The Word, National Centre for the Written Word, South Tyneside

![The Word, South Shields](assets/the_word_south_shields.jpg)

Opened in October 2016 as the centrepiece of South Tyneside Council’s £100m regeneration of South Shields town centre, **The Word** combines a central library with exhibition galleries, digital media labs, a gaming zone, and a rooftop viewing terrace.

In its first six months of operation, The Word attracted over 225,000 visitors, with library loans and new member registrations increasing dramatically. It demonstrates how bold investment in cultural and library infrastructure can stimulate town centre regeneration and civic pride.

## Planning public library services to meet local needs

Under Section 7 of the Public Libraries and Museums Act 1964, local library authorities in England have a statutory duty to provide a ‘comprehensive and efficient’ public library service for all persons desiring to make use of it.

While the Act does not define exact prescriptive standards, judicial reviews and government guidance have established clear principles:

- ‘Comprehensive’ does not mean providing everything that everyone could possibly want, but rather meeting the general needs of the community and specific needs of adults and children.
- ‘Efficient’ relates to making the best use of available resources to deliver high-quality outcomes.
- Decisions must be based on a robust assessment of local needs, equality impact assessments, and genuine public consultation.

<!-- PAGE: 19 -->

### Taking a Strategic, Long-Term Approach

A successful library strategy requires:

1. **Robust Needs Assessment:** Analyzing demographic trends, deprivation indices, reading habits, digital access rates, and travel patterns across the authority.
2. **Clear Outcomes Alignment:** Demonstrating how the library service contributes directly to council corporate priorities, local health strategies, and economic plans.
3. **Evidence-Based Option Appraisal:** Evaluating alternative delivery models, co-location opportunities, and technology upgrades objectively against cost and quality criteria.
4. **Comprehensive Consultation:** Engaging with library users, non-users, staff, and partner organisations to understand community aspirations and concerns.
5. **Robust Equality Impact Assessments (EIAs):** Identifying potential impacts on protected characteristic groups under the Equality Act 2010 and establishing appropriate mitigating actions.

<!-- PAGE: 20 -->

### Evidence-Based Planning Tools

Councillors and officers should utilise available national datasets and benchmarking resources:

- CIPFA Public Library Statistics (actuals and estimates)
- Libraries Taskforce Toolkits and guidance notes
- Census data and Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD)
- Local public health profiles and Joint Strategic Needs Assessments (JSNAs)
- Arts Council England Active Lives survey data.

<!-- PAGE: 21 -->

### Avoiding Pitfalls and Legal Challenges

Several high-profile judicial reviews (e.g. Gloucestershire, Somerset, and Lincolnshire) have highlighted the legal risks of poorly planned library reorganisations. Key lessons include:

- Decisions cannot be made purely on financial targets without assessing community needs.
- Consultation must be undertaken at a formative stage with an open mind, providing adequate information and time for responses.
- The Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED) under Section 149 of the Equality Act 2010 must be conscientiously exercised with rigor and documented evidence.
- The statutory duty under the 1964 Act remains with the council even if services are transferred to community groups or alternative delivery models.

<!-- PAGE: 22 -->

### Case Study: London Borough of Sutton

Sutton Council undertook an extensive, evidence-based review of its library service to modernise provision and address budget pressures. Rather than proposing closures, the council:

- invested in digital infrastructure and automated self-service
- expanded partnership working with colleges and community groups
- co-located council services within libraries to create shared community hubs
- secured political and community consensus through transparent public dialogue.

<!-- PAGE: 23 -->

### Case Study: Bradford Metropolitan District Council

Bradford restructured its library service to align with early years development and literacy cold spots. By focusing resources on mobile library routes, community hubs, and school readiness programmes in high-deprivation wards, the council ensured that vulnerable children and families retained access to high-quality literacy provision.

<!-- PAGE: 24 -->

### Case Study: Suffolk Libraries IPS

In 2012, Suffolk County Council became the first local authority in England to transfer its entire public library service to an independent, employee-led industrial and provident society (IPS) with charitable status: **Suffolk Libraries**.

The council commissions Suffolk Libraries under a long-term contract to deliver statutory library provision. In its first five years, Suffolk Libraries:

- kept all 44 branch libraries and mobile services open
- saved the council over 30% in operating costs
- increased user satisfaction, staff engagement, and community involvement
- established local community groups for every branch library across the county.

## Helping libraries to use better evidence to support decision making

Effective decision making requires continuous monitoring and evaluation. Councillors should ensure that their authority tracks:

- traditional transactional metrics: visits, physical loans, digital issues, public computer bookings
- outcome metrics: health and wellbeing improvements, digital skill accreditations, early years development milestones
- customer feedback and satisfaction surveys
- financial performance, unit costs, and external funding leverage.

<!-- PAGE: 26 -->

## Considering different models for local service delivery

Councils across England deliver library services through a variety of governance and operational models:

1. **In-house service delivery:** Direct council operation, often restructured into integrated directorates (e.g. customer services, culture, or communities).
2. **Shared services:** Merging library operations or back-office management across two or more local authorities to achieve economies of scale (e.g. the Tri-borough library partnership in London).
3. **Public sector mutuals / Trusts:** Employee-led or community-led independent mutuals operating under contract to the council (e.g. Suffolk Libraries, Explore York, Libraries Unlimited in Devon).
4. **Commercial partnerships:** Contracting service delivery or facility management to external commercial operators (e.g. Greenwich Leisure Limited - GLL).
5. **Community-managed models:** Transferring operational responsibility for individual branch libraries to local voluntary groups.

<!-- PAGE: 27 -->

## Community-managed and community-supported libraries

Community involvement in libraries has grown significantly across England. It is essential to distinguish between different models:

- **Independent library:** Run entirely by volunteers and a community organisation, with no ongoing operational support or funding from the local authority; sits outside the statutory service.
- **Co-produced library:** Community-led with some continuing council support (e.g. access to the council's Library Management System, book stock rotation, and professional staff liaison); may form part of the statutory network.
- **Council-led community supported library:** Council remains responsible for the building, stock, and statutory compliance, with volunteers assisting paid staff to extend opening hours or deliver activities.

<!-- PAGE: 28 -->

### Key Considerations for Community Libraries

Before supporting a transition to community management, councillors should assess:

- **Capacity and sustainability:** Does the community organisation have the volunteer numbers, leadership skills, and financial resources to maintain long-term operations?
- **Equitable access:** Will the library remain genuinely free, open, and welcoming to all members of the public?
- **Safeguarding and legal compliance:** Are appropriate safeguarding policies, health and safety procedures, and data protection controls in place?
- **Council support:** What ongoing support will the council provide in terms of stock rotation, IT maintenance, and professional librarian advice?

<!-- PAGE: 29 -->

### Checklist: Transitioning to an Alternative Delivery Model

When evaluating alternative delivery models, ask:

- What are the primary objectives: financial savings, service improvement, greater flexibility, or community empowerment?
- Does the business case objectively analyze initial transition costs, VAT implications, pensions (LGPS), and ongoing procurement liabilities?
- How will the council exercise democratic accountability, contract management, and statutory oversight under the 1964 Act?
- How have staff and trade unions been engaged in the transition process?

<!-- PAGE: 30 -->

## Funding library services in varied and sustainable ways

Councils are exploring diverse income streams to supplement core council tax funding:

- **Developer contributions:** Section 106 agreements and the Community Infrastructure Levy (CIL) to fund new library facilities, capital refurbishment, and initial book stock in growth areas.
- **External grant funding:** Arts Council England (National Lottery Project Grants, Library Improvement Fund), National Lottery Heritage Fund, and philanthropic trusts.
- **Commercial income:** Room hire, event ticket sales, cafes, retail sales, and passport/visa verification services.
- **Fundraising and donations:** Individual giving, friends organisations, and corporate sponsorship.
- **Social investment and community shares:** Utilising social impact bonds or community share issues to support capital redevelopment.

<!-- PAGE: 31 -->

### Case Study: Woolwich Centre Library, Royal Borough of Greenwich

Opened in 2011, the Woolwich Centre co-located a major central library with council customer service centre, offices for 2,000 council staff, and community facilities. By consolidating 33 administrative buildings into one, the council achieved significant efficiency savings while creating one of the busiest libraries in London.

<!-- PAGE: 32 -->

### Maximising Planning Developer Contributions (S106 and CIL)

Councillors should ensure that libraries are embedded within their authority’s Local Plan and Infrastructure Delivery Plan (IDP):

- Identify specific library infrastructure requirements generated by housing developments.
- Secure Section 106 contributions for book stock replenishment, digital hardware, and physical space expansion.
- Ensure that CIL regulation lists include library facilities as eligible strategic infrastructure.

<!-- PAGE: 33 -->

## Stronger coordination and partnership working

Libraries are natural collaborative spaces. Strong local partnerships enhance the library offer while achieving mutual goals:

- **Health and Wellbeing Boards / Clinical Commissioning Groups (CCGs):** Commissioning health literacy, social prescribing, and dementia support in libraries.
- **Colleges and Adult Education Providers:** Co-delivering literacy, numeracy, and ESOL classes in library learning spaces.
- **Police and Emergency Services:** Using libraries for neighbourhood policing drop-ins and crime prevention advice.
- **Local Business Networks and Chambers of Commerce:** Linking entrepreneurs with BIPC business resources and meeting spaces.

<!-- PAGE: 34 -->

### Regional Consortia and Library Management Systems (LMS)

Joining regional library consortia (such as the London Libraries Consortium or South Western Regional Library System) enables authorities to:

- share catalogue databases, giving residents access to millions of titles
- procure books and e-resources collaboratively at substantial volume discounts
- reduce software licensing and IT maintenance overheads.

<!-- PAGE: 35 -->

### Case Study: Warwickshire County Council

Warwickshire developed an integrated community hub strategy that brought together libraries, children's centres, and adult education into multi-agency community hubs. The model preserved local services in rural market towns and urban neighbourhoods while reducing property overheads.

<!-- PAGE: 36 -->

### Shared Services and Inter-Authority Collaboration

Councils facing severe budget pressures should examine opportunities for shared leadership and joint management arrangements with neighbouring authorities. While requiring upfront legal and cultural alignment, shared services can protect front-line branch networks by streamlining senior management and back-office functions.

<!-- PAGE: 37 -->

## Developing the library workforce, now and for the future

A modern library service relies on a skilled, confident, and versatile workforce. The role of library staff has transformed from passive collection gatekeepers to proactive community facilitators, digital navigators, and event coordinators.

Councillors should ensure that their service invests in:

- **Continuing Professional Development (CPD):** Training in digital skills, customer care, safeguarding, and reader development.
- **Leadership Development:** Equipping future service leaders through national schemes like the Libraries Connected Leading Libraries programme.
- **New Entry Routes:** Developing apprenticeships, including the Level 3 Library, Information and Archive Services (LIAS) Assistant standard.
- **Diversity:** Ensuring that the library workforce and volunteer base reflect the cultural and demographic diversity of the community.

<!-- PAGE: 38 -->

### The Role of Volunteers

Volunteers provide valuable support to public libraries, enriching activities through reading groups, digital buddying, and local history research.

However, councillors must recognise the distinction between volunteers and paid staff:

- Volunteers should complement, enhance, and extend the work of paid professionals, not replace core staffing.
- Effective volunteer programmes require professional management, coordination, training, and safeguarding oversight.

<!-- PAGE: 39 -->

### Digital Skills and Digital Inclusion

Over 10 million adults in the UK lack basic digital skills. Libraries are on the frontline of tackling the digital divide:

- providing free access to public computers, high-speed internet, and printing
- hosting ‘digital buddies’ and one-to-one digital support sessions
- helping residents complete online universal credit claims, passport renewals, and council service requests.

<!-- PAGE: 40 -->

### Safe and Welcoming Environments

Libraries are trusted civic spaces. Maintaining a safe, welcoming, and inclusive environment requires clear operational policies on acceptable behavior, trained staff capable of de-escalating challenging situations, and strong partnerships with local community policing teams.

<!-- PAGE: 41 -->

## Bringing it all together

Transforming a public library service is a journey that requires sustained political leadership, strategic clarity, and authentic community engagement.

Councillors should ensure that:

- the library service has a clear, written strategy adopted by full council or cabinet
- service transformation is communicated proactively to residents and stakeholders
- performance and social outcomes are regularly reported and celebrated
- the service remains adaptable and responsive to emerging community needs.

<!-- PAGE: 42 -->

### Case Study: Norfolk County Council

Norfolk Library Service pioneered the deployment of **Open+** automated technology, enabling registered users to access library facilities during unstaffed hours using their library card and PIN.

Combined with targeted staffed hours, mobile library outreach, and strong volunteer support, Open+ enabled Norfolk to significantly increase weekly opening hours, expanding access for working families, students, and rural residents.

## Scrutinising and championing the delivery plan

### Defining what an excellent library service looks like

An excellent 21st-century public library service demonstrates:

- **Strong community engagement:** High levels of active membership and participation across all age groups and demographics.
- **Clear strategic vision:** Direct contribution to local economic, health, educational, and social outcomes.
- **Vibrant physical and digital spaces:** Well-maintained, flexible buildings and comprehensive 24/7 online collections.
- **Professional excellence:** Dedicated, highly trained staff supported by an active volunteer network.
- **Financial resilience:** Innovative funding partnerships and efficient shared services.

<!-- PAGE: 44 -->

### Effective scrutiny of public library services

Overview and scrutiny committees have a vital role in testing and shaping library policy:

- Pre-decision scrutiny: Reviewing needs assessments, consultation feedback, and options appraisals before executive decisions are finalised.
- Performance monitoring: Scrutinising KPIs, footfall trends, and budget variances.
- Deep dives: Investigating specific topics such as digital inclusion, early years reading, or alternative delivery models.
- Amplifying community voices: Ensuring that feedback from young people, older residents, and marginalised communities is heard.
